# Aerobic Development

Deep reference for the physiology and the periodisation reasoning behind the run build.
`SKILL.md` holds the decision procedure — which session to write today, which flag blocks it.
This file holds the *why*: what actually improves, how fast, in what order, and what happens
when the order is wrong. It is written for an athlete returning to running in January after 22
weeks of lifting with only maintenance aerobic work, so the bias throughout is towards tissue
tolerance over fitness.

## 1. What actually makes someone faster

Endurance performance at any distance beyond about 1500 m reduces to four physiological
determinants (Joyner 1991; Joyner & Coyle 2008). Velocity at any given duration is essentially
VO₂max × fractional utilisation ÷ oxygen cost of running. Everything programmed below is aimed
at one of these four, and if a session can't be assigned to one, it doesn't belong in the week.

| Determinant | What it is | What trains it | Trainability |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VO₂max** | Ceiling on aerobic ATP production; central (cardiac output) and peripheral (extraction) | 3-8 min intervals at 95-100% vVO₂max; large easy volume in the untrained | High in novices (15-25%), low in trained (~5%). Plateaus early |
| **Lactate / anaerobic threshold** | The highest intensity sustainable in metabolic steady state (LT2 / MLSS / critical speed) | Tempo, cruise intervals, threshold blocks; large sub-threshold volume | High and remains trainable for years. The main lever in trained athletes |
| **Fractional utilisation** | What % of VO₂max can be held for the race duration | Long runs, sustained sub-threshold work, race-pace volume | High. Distance-specific — it is what long runs actually buy |
| **Running economy** | Oxygen (or energy) cost per metre at a given speed | Heavy strength training, plyometrics, sprint mechanics, accumulated running years, footwear | Moderate (2-8%), slow, and driven more by strength and neuromuscular work than by running volume |

Two things follow that matter for programming.

**VO₂max is the least useful target for an experienced athlete.** It is the determinant with
the lowest headroom and the highest cost — VO₂max work is the most fatiguing, most injurious
intensity available, and in a trained runner it buys a few per cent at best (Midgley et al.
2007). It earns a place late in a build, in small doses, not as the engine of the plan. The
threshold and fractional utilisation are where an experienced runner's remaining gains live.

**Economy is where lifting pays.** Heavy resistance training improves running economy by
roughly 2-8% without increasing VO₂max (Rønnestad & Mujika 2014; Balsalobre-Fernández et al.
2016; Blagrove et al. 2018). This athlete has just spent 22 weeks accumulating exactly that
adaptation. It will not be visible in the first four weeks of running — economy gains express
through *running* mechanics and need running to surface — but it is banked, and it is the
reason the lifting continues at 2×/week through the build rather than being dropped.

### What the limiter actually is on return from a layoff

Be blunt about this, because the athlete will draw the wrong conclusion from how good the first
three weeks feel.

| Quality | State after 22 weeks off running | Recovery speed |
|---|---|---|
| Running economy | Largely preserved; improved if strength work transferred | Fast — weeks. Neuromuscular pattern returns quickly |
| Threshold (as % of current VO₂max) | Preserved as a ratio, lower in absolute pace | Fast — 4-8 weeks |
| VO₂max | Down, but not catastrophically given maintenance cycling | Moderate — 6-12 weeks |
| Aerobic base (mitochondrial density, capillarisation) | Substantially reduced in the running-specific musculature | Slow — months |
| Connective-tissue load tolerance (tendon, bone, fascia) | The largest deficit of all, and the least perceptible | Slowest — months, and asymptomatic until it fails |

**The limiter on return is not fitness. It is tissue.** The athlete will be able to hold paces
that their tendons and bones have no recent history of absorbing, and the sensation of running
easily is not evidence that the tissue is coping. Section 8 develops this; it is the single
most important idea in this file.

## 2. Intensity zones, and how to set them

### The two models

Use both. The 5-zone model is for prescribing sessions; the 3-zone model is for auditing the
distribution of a week or a block. They answer different questions and the mapping between them
is where most self-coached athletes go wrong.

| 5-zone | Name | % HRmax | % LTHR | RPE (1-10) | Talk test | Typical continuous duration | 3-zone |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Z1 | Recovery | 60-72% | <82% | 1-2 | Full conversation, nasal breathing possible | 20-45 min | Zone 1 |
| Z2 | Aerobic / easy | 72-82% | 82-89% | 3-4 | Full sentences, slightly interrupted | 40 min-3 h | Zone 1 |
| Z3 | Tempo / marathon | 82-88% | 90-94% | 5-6 | Short sentences only | 20-75 min | Zone 2 |
| Z4 | Threshold | 88-93% | 95-102% | 7-8 | A few words at a time | 8-20 min per rep, 20-40 min total | Zone 2 (upper) / Zone 3 |
| Z5 | VO₂max and above | 93-100%+ | >102% (HR lags) | 9-10 | Single words or none | 2-6 min per rep, 12-25 min total | Zone 3 |

The **3-zone model** is defined by the two physiological turn points, not by HR percentages:
Zone 1 is below LT1 / VT1 (the first lactate rise, roughly where conversation starts to break),
Zone 2 is between LT1 and LT2, and Zone 3 is above LT2 / critical speed. When people quote
"80/20", this is the model they are quoting, and **tempo running counts as Zone 2, not as
easy** — that reclassification is what makes most self-reported 80/20 weeks fictitious.

### Anchoring the zones without a lab

Ranked by how much to trust them for this athlete in the first mesocycle.

| Method | Protocol | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Recent race / time trial pace** | Convert a recent 5K-10K result to training paces (Daniels' VDOT tables or an equivalent) | Most reliable early because it anchors to *pace*, not to a physiological signal that is still recalibrating. No recent race means no anchor — do not guess |
| **30-min TT for LTHR** | 30 min all-out solo effort on flat ground; **average HR of the final 20 min ≈ LTHR** (Friel). Average pace of the full 30 min approximates threshold pace | The standard field protocol. Do not run this in the first 4-6 weeks back — it is a maximal effort on untrained tissue, and the hamstring flag makes an all-out solo TT a poor early choice |
| **Critical speed from two efforts** | Two maximal efforts of clearly different duration (e.g. 3 min and 12 min); the asymptote of the speed-duration relationship approximates the Zone 2/3 boundary (Jones & Vanhatalo 2017) | Physiologically the cleanest field estimate of LT2. Same objection: it needs two maximal efforts |
| **Talk test** | Can hold a full sentence = Zone 1. Short phrases = Zone 2. Words only = Zone 3 | Crude and remarkably robust; corresponds well to the ventilatory thresholds (Foster and colleagues). This is the primary anchor for weeks 1-4 |
| **HRmax-derived percentages** | 220-age and its variants | Population formulas carry a standard deviation of roughly ±10-12 bpm. Use only as a sanity check, never as a prescription |

### Why heart rate is unreliable early — and what to use instead

HR is a downstream, confounded signal. It answers "what is the cardiovascular system doing
right now", which is not the same question as "what training stimulus is this". It is
specifically untrustworthy in five situations, four of which apply to this athlete in January.

| Situation | What happens | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| First 2-3 weeks back | Plasma volume is low, stroke volume is reduced, so HR at any given pace is elevated 5-15 bpm | Following HR zones drives the pace absurdly slow, or worse, the athlete assumes the zones are wrong and ignores them |
| Heat and humidity | Cardiovascular drift from thermoregulatory demand; HR rises at constant pace and workload | Zone 2 by HR becomes Zone 1 by pace. Real cost, but it is a heat cost, not an aerobic one |
| Hills and undulating ground | HR lags the effort change by 30-90 s and never settles on rolling terrain | Interval and tempo control by HR fails outright on hills. Use pace, power, or RPE |
| Under-recovered / poor sleep / illness | HR can be suppressed *or* elevated depending on the mechanism | Suppressed HR under fatigue is the dangerous case — it reads as "fresh" and permits a session that should not happen |
| High-intensity intervals | HR takes 60-120 s to reach steady state; short reps end before it arrives | Never prescribe reps under ~3 min by HR |

**For the first three weeks back, pace and RPE anchor every session and HR is recorded but does
not control anything.** From roughly week 4, once plasma volume has re-expanded, HR becomes
useful again — and its most valuable use from then on is not as a prescription but as a
monitoring signal (section 9). The failure mode is the athlete strapping themselves to a HR
ceiling in week 2, walking up every hill to protect it, and concluding they have lost more
fitness than they have.

## 3. Intensity distribution

### The three shapes

| Model | 3-zone shape | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Polarised** | ~75-85% Zone 1, <10% Zone 2, ~10-20% Zone 3 | High-volume athletes; VO₂max-oriented blocks; the observed pattern in elite endurance sport |
| **Pyramidal** | ~75-85% Zone 1, ~10-15% Zone 2, ~5-10% Zone 3 | Base and build phases, marathon and longer, sub-elite athletes on moderate volume |
| **Threshold-heavy** | ~50-65% Zone 1, ~25-40% Zone 2, small Zone 3 | Rarely deliberate. Usually the accidental result of no intensity discipline |

### What Seiler's 80/20 actually says

Seiler & Kjerland (2006) and Seiler (2010) observed that elite endurance athletes across
several sports distribute roughly 75-80% of their *training sessions* below the first
ventilatory threshold and 15-20% above the second. Three qualifications change how the finding
should be used:

1. It is **session count**, not minutes. Counted by time, the low-intensity fraction in elite
   athletes is typically higher still — often 85-90% — because the easy sessions are long and
   the hard ones are short.
2. It emerged **descriptively**. It is a report of what athletes who train 15-25 h/week
   converged on, not a controlled comparison. It is a strong observation, not an experimental
   result.
3. It was observed in athletes whose absolute volume is 3-5× this athlete's. The rationale for
   so much easy running is partly that it is the only intensity at which that volume is
   survivable.

The controlled evidence is more mixed than the slogan suggests. Esteve-Lanao et al. (2007)
found a sub-elite running group at ~80/20 outperformed a more threshold-weighted group. Stöggl
& Sperlich (2014) found polarised superior to threshold, high-volume, and HIIT-only blocks over
nine weeks. Against that, descriptive work on world-class distance runners reports
pyramidal distributions dominating many elite programmes, and reviews of sub-elite athletes generally find
pyramidal at least as effective as polarised. **For a sub-elite athlete on 5-8 h/week,
pyramidal is the honest default**, with the distribution drifting more polarised only in a late
VO₂max-oriented block.

### The rule that actually matters

The distinction between polarised and pyramidal is second order. The first-order rule is:

**The easy days must actually be easy, and the hard days must actually be hard.**

Both models agree on the large easy fraction and differ only in how the remaining 20% is split.
Get the 80% right and the argument about the 20% is close to irrelevant.

**The failure mode is the moderate-intensity rut.** Every run drifts to the same
comfortably-hard effort — Zone 3 by feel, a bit above easy, a bit below threshold. It is the
most seductive place to train because it feels productive and it is not painful. It produces a
specific pattern: the easy runs are too fast to allow recovery, so the hard sessions are run
under residual fatigue and fail to reach the intensity that would drive adaptation. Nothing
gets a clean stimulus, volume plateaus because recovery never completes, and progress stalls at
exactly the point the athlete is trying hardest. It is also the distribution most strongly
associated with the running-related injury pattern of accumulated load with no recovery days.

Diagnose it by looking at the pace distribution over four weeks. If it is unimodal — a single
hump with no clear separation between easy running and quality — the athlete is in the rut. The
fix is not more discipline on hard days. It is slowing the easy days down, by 45-75 s/km if
necessary, and tolerating how slow that feels for three weeks.

For this athlete in the first mesocycle, the distribution is close to **100% Zone 1**, because
there is no intensity at all until the tissue-build criteria in section 6 are met. The
distribution question does not arise until week 5 or later.

## 4. The long run

### What it is for

Not distance. The long run's purpose is a stack of adaptations that only sustained duration
produces: mitochondrial biogenesis and capillarisation in the recruited fibres, increased
intramuscular glycogen storage, improved fat oxidation at a given intensity, plasma volume
expansion, cardiac stroke volume, and — the one that matters most and is most easily forgotten
— **progressive connective-tissue and bone loading tolerance under fatigue**. Late in a long
run, recruitment shifts as the primary fibres fatigue, and the athlete trains the ability to
hold mechanics with tired muscle. That last quality is what fails in the closing quarter of a
race, and it is trained by duration, not by pace.

Fractional utilisation — how much of VO₂max can be sustained for the race duration — is the
determinant the long run buys, and it is the one that is most distance-specific.

### Progression rules

| Rule | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | Once per week; every 10-14 days when volume is low or the week is heavy | Recovery cost is disproportionate; two long runs a week is a load-management error at this volume |
| Step size | +10-15 min, or +1.5-3 km, per progression | Small enough that a bad step is recoverable; the long run is the largest single acute load of the week and the most common site of a load spike |
| Progression cadence | Progress 2-3 weeks, then hold or cut ~25-30% for one week | Matches the 3:1 weekly structure in section 5 — the long run should deload when the week deloads |
| Ceiling as % of week | **25-30% of weekly volume; hard cap ~35%** | See below |
| Duration ceiling | ~2.5-3 h regardless of goal | Beyond ~3 h the recovery and tissue cost rises faster than the adaptation. Additional fractional-utilisation work is better bought with a mid-week medium-long run |
| Pace | Zone 1-2 throughout, until quality is earned | Running the long run hard converts the week's biggest volume block into the week's biggest fatigue block |

### How long is long enough

| Goal | Long run target | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 5K | 60-80 min | Sufficient for the aerobic support; the specific work is elsewhere |
| 10K | 75-95 min | Duration matters more than pace at this distance |
| Half marathon | 100-120 min, or ~18-22 km | Reaching race duration is achievable, so build to it |
| Marathon | 2:15-3:00, or ~28-34 km | Time-capped, not distance-capped. A 3:30 long run to "cover the distance" costs a week of training and buys very little |
| Trail / ultra | 3-5 h, terrain-specific, occasionally back-to-back | Vertical gain and descent load substitute for flat distance; descent is the injury risk, not ascent |

### The percentage-of-volume rule

The long run should sit at roughly **25-30% of weekly volume**. A 40 km week supports a 10-12
km long run. The common error is the inverse: a 25 km week containing a 16 km long run, which
is 64% of the week's load in a single session with nothing supporting it.

Above roughly 35%, injury risk climbs. The mechanism is a load spike: a single session that
greatly exceeds anything the tissue has recently absorbed, which is the pattern most
consistently associated with running injury in the progression literature (Nielsen et al. 2014,
where weekly progressions above ~30% were associated with distance-related injuries). The
percentage rule is a heuristic, not a measured threshold — but it enforces the right behaviour,
which is that **the way to get a longer long run is to build the rest of the week first.**

### When to add quality inside it

Only after the long run duration itself is stable for 3-4 weeks and the goal race is half
marathon or longer. Then, in order of aggressiveness:

1. **Progression finish** — final 15-20 min lifted from Zone 1 to Zone 2. Lowest cost, teaches
   pacing discipline.
2. **Marathon-pace blocks** — e.g. 3 × 10 min at Zone 3 inside the run, with easy running
   between.
3. **Fatigued threshold** — a short Zone 4 block late in the run. Highest specificity, highest
   cost, latest in the build, and only when the hamstring is fully cleared.

**No quality inside the long run while the proximal hamstring is a live flag.** Fast running at
the end of a long run is high-velocity hip flexion on fatigued tissue with degraded mechanics,
which is the exact mechanism of proximal hamstring injury.

## 5. Volume, and how it should be built

### Weekly volume by goal

Ranges assume a build phase, not week one.

| Goal | Weekly volume | Runs/week | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Return to running / general | 20-40 km | 3-4 | The first 6-8 weeks live here regardless of the eventual goal |
| 5K-10K | 40-65 km | 4-5 | Volume still matters more than most 5K runners believe |
| Half marathon | 50-75 km | 4-6 | The medium-long mid-week run appears here |
| Marathon | 65-100 km | 5-6 | Below ~55 km/week the marathon becomes a survival exercise |
| Trail / ultra | Track by **time and vertical gain**, not distance | 4-6 | 6-10 h/week; distance is a poor load proxy on gradient |

### The 10% rule has poor evidence

The "increase weekly volume by no more than 10%" rule is folklore with a specific, unsupportive
evidence base. Buist et al. (2008) randomised novice runners to a graded 13-week programme
against a standard 8-week programme and found **no difference in running-related injury
incidence** — the more gradual progression did not reduce injuries. Systematic reviews of
training-error research have repeatedly failed to establish 10% as a meaningful threshold.

Two reasons it fails in practice: it is a *percentage*, so it is far too permissive at high
volume (10% of a 90 km week is 9 km) and far too restrictive at low volume (10% of a 20 km week
is 2 km, below measurement noise); and it says nothing about the *distribution* of that volume
within the week, which is where the actual risk lives — a load spike inside a compliant week is
still a spike.

### What to use instead

| Rule | Detail | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **3 weeks up, 1 week down** | Three progressive weeks, then a week at ~60-70% of the peak week | Fatigue accumulates faster than it dissipates. The down week converts accumulated stimulus into realised adaptation — the same logic as the lifting deload |
| **~5-10% per step, on the 3-week rolling average** | Compare the new week against the trailing 3-week mean, not against last week | Removes the ratchet effect where a down week becomes the new baseline and the next "10%" is really 40% |
| **Progress time on feet before pace** | Add duration first; add speed only once duration is stable | Mechanical load per step scales steeply with velocity. Ground reaction force, tendon strain rate, and peak hamstring strain all rise faster than pace does. A 10% duration increase at easy pace is a far smaller tissue insult than a 10% pace increase over the same duration |
| **Progress one variable at a time** | Volume, intensity, or long-run duration — never two in the same week | When something hurts, the cause is identifiable. This is the same "never add load and range in the same week" rule from the hamstring guardrails |
| **Hold the week after any missed week** | Illness, travel, or a flare resets the baseline | The tissue detrains faster than the fitness feeling does |

### Frequency beats single long sessions when rebuilding

Four 30-minute runs and two 60-minute runs are the same weekly volume and a different stimulus.
When rebuilding tissue, the higher-frequency option is better on three counts: each session's
peak load is lower, so the risk of exceeding tissue tolerance in any one session is lower; the
mechanical loading signal that drives tendon and bone adaptation responds to *frequency* of
loading cycles more than to duration of any one bout, with a saturating response within a
session; and the running gait pattern is re-learned faster with more exposures. Frequency also
converts better to the run-walk progressions that section 6's first mesocycle depends on.

The practical version: **when adding volume in the first two mesocycles, add a run before you
lengthen a run.**

## 6. Periodisation models

### The three shapes

| Model | Structure | Fits when |
|---|---|---|
| **Linear / traditional** | Base (volume, low intensity) → Build (threshold, race-specific) → Peak (VO₂max, sharpening) → Taper | The default. Long runway (12+ weeks), one goal race, and — critically — an athlete who needs the base phase anyway |
| **Block** | Concentrated mesocycles each loading one quality hard (e.g. 3 weeks VO₂max-dominant, then 4 weeks threshold-dominant), with the others held at maintenance | Trained athletes with a stable base and a short runway; multiple races. Needs high recoverability and a tissue base that can absorb concentrated intensity — this athlete will not have that until spring |
| **Reverse** | Intensity first at low volume, volume added later | Short-course goals; athletes with severe time constraints early; a base built by cross-training. Attractive on paper here, and **wrong for this athlete** — it front-loads intensity onto the least prepared tissue in the calendar |

### The 12-week shape (half marathon or 10K goal, athlete with a base)

| Weeks | Mesocycle | Focus | Distribution (3-zone) | Long run |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1-4 | Base | Volume, easy running, strides from week 3 | ~95 / 5 / 0 | 25% → 28% of week |
| 5-8 | Build | Threshold introduced; volume held or up ~5% | ~80 / 15 / 5 | Hold, add progression finish |
| 9-11 | Sharpen | Race-specific pace; VO₂max touches; volume down ~10% | ~78 / 12 / 10 | Cut to ~22% of week |
| 12 | Taper | Volume down 40-50%, intensity retained at reduced volume | ~80 / 10 / 10 | ~50% of peak long run |

### The 16-week shape — this athlete, January start

The important structural difference: **the first mesocycle is a tissue build, not a fitness
build.** Its success criterion is not a pace or a volume — it is four consecutive symptom-free
weeks. Nothing in weeks 5-16 is permitted to start until that criterion is met, and if it
slips, everything downstream slips with it.

| Weeks | Mesocycle | Purpose | Sessions | Rules |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1-4 | **Tissue build** | Load tolerance, gait re-acquisition, connective-tissue exposure | 3-4 runs, 20-35 min, run-walk if needed. All Zone 1 | No intensity. No hills. No consecutive running days in weeks 1-2. Volume progresses by frequency, not duration |
| 5-8 | Aerobic base | Volume, easy running, first neuromuscular touches | 4 runs, one becoming a long run. Uphill strides (4-6 × 8-10 s at 6-8%) once the base is continuous | **Uphill strides before flat ones** — the gradient shortens the stride and cuts peak hamstring length at peak force. Flat strides wait until three clean uphill sessions have banked. 3:1 weekly structure begins |
| 9-12 | Build | Threshold as the primary quality | 4-5 runs; one threshold session, one long run with a progression finish | One quality session per week only, until two consecutive weeks show clean recovery. Then two |
| 13-15 | Specific | Race-pace work; small VO₂max dose in the final weeks | 5 runs; two quality sessions; volume trimmed ~10% | Fastest running of the whole build sits here — deliberately, after 12 weeks of tissue preparation |
| 16 | Taper | Reduce volume, hold intensity | 4 runs, all short, quality retained at ~1/3 volume | Cut volume, never frequency. Losing frequency in the taper makes the athlete feel stale |

Weeks 1-4 will feel insultingly easy to someone who has been training hard for five months. Say
so in advance, because the athlete who is not warned will add to them. The purpose of that
mesocycle is to accumulate loading cycles on tendon and bone at a rate they can remodel to, and
there is no version of it that can be shortened by feeling good.

## 7. Concurrent training with lifting

### The interference effect, correctly scoped

Concurrent aerobic and resistance training attenuates strength and hypertrophy adaptation
relative to resistance training alone. Wilson et al. (2012) meta-analysed this and identified
the moderators that actually matter:

| Moderator | Finding | Programming consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Modality** | Running interferes more than cycling, with a larger effect on hypertrophy and lower-body strength | Expect the lifting to feel harder once running starts. Cycling is the low-interference cross-training option |
| **Duration** | Interference scales with session duration; long sessions are the problem | The long run is the highest-interference session of the week |
| **Frequency** | More endurance sessions per week, more interference | 4-5 runs/week is a real endurance dose. Lifting expectations should be maintenance, not progression |
| **Intensity** | High-intensity endurance work interferes more per minute | Never place a hard interval session adjacent to the heavy lower-body lift |

Interference runs mainly in one direction: **endurance training blunts strength adaptation far
more than strength training blunts endurance adaptation.** That asymmetry is the whole basis
for keeping the lifting in.

### Why lifting helps the running

Heavy strength training (≥80% 1RM, low reps) and plyometrics improve running economy by roughly
2-8% with no change in VO₂max or body mass (Rønnestad & Mujika 2014; Balsalobre-Fernández et
al. 2016; Blagrove et al. 2018). The mechanism is neuromuscular and elastic — improved rate of
force development, greater musculotendinous stiffness, better use of stored elastic energy —
not hypertrophic. Beyond economy, Lauersen et al. (2014) found that strength training almost
halved overuse injuries and reduced all sports injuries to under a third — which, for an athlete
carrying a hamstring flag, is the more relevant finding.

**Keep the two lifting sessions. They are not competing with the run build; they are part of
it.** The one adjustment is expectation: hold loads, accept slower progression, and do not
chase PBs during a running build.

### Session ordering and separation

| Rule | Detail | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Same day beats adjacent days** | Stack the hard run and the heavy lift on the same day, leaving the next day genuinely easy | Preserves the hard/easy structure of the week. Splitting them across two days produces two medium days and no recovery day |
| **Priority quality goes first** | Whichever adaptation the phase prioritises is the first session of the day, on fresh legs | In a run build, the run leads. In the transition weeks, the lift may lead |
| **≥6 h between sessions when both are hard** | Robineau et al. (2016) found longer recovery between concurrent sessions preserved adaptation better than back-to-back | Morning run, evening lift is the workable pattern |
| **≥24 h between heavy posterior-chain lifting and any fast running** | RDLs, Nordics, and heavy hip hinges leave the hamstring in a compromised state for 24-48 h | This is the highest-risk adjacency in the whole week for this athlete |
| **Never a heavy lower-body lift the day before the long run** | Fatigued hamstrings and glutes degrade mechanics precisely when duration is longest | Long-run mechanics failing under fatigue is the reinjury mechanism |
| **Upper-body lifting is nearly free** | Place it anywhere, including the day before a quality run | The interference is modality- and limb-specific |

### Sample week — build phase, 2 lifts and 5 runs

| Day | Session 1 | Session 2 | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Lift — lower emphasis | — | Follows the Sunday long run, so the hard day is protected and Tuesday's easy run is on 24 h |
| Tue | Easy run 40 min, Zone 1 | — | Genuinely easy. Flat route |
| Wed | Quality run — threshold | — | Fresh legs, 48 h from the lower lift |
| Thu | Easy run 35-40 min | Lift — upper emphasis | Upper lifting does not interfere with tomorrow's running |
| Fri | Easy run 40 min or rest | — | The flex day. Drop it first when anything is sore |
| Sat | Easy run 30 min + strides | — | Neuromuscular touch, low volume, primes Sunday |
| Sun | Long run | — | The week's largest single load, on the day with the most recovery either side |

Early in the build, the Wednesday quality slot is an easy run and the Saturday strides are
absent — the same skeleton, no intensity in it. Adding intensity is a promotion of that one
slot, not a restructuring of the week.

**Never let the week hold two quality runs and two heavy lower-body lifts simultaneously.**
Four hard sessions on 2 recovery days is the arithmetic that produces overuse injury. If a
second quality run enters in weeks 13-15, one lift goes to maintenance intensity that week.

## 8. Detraining and retraining

### What is lost over 22 weeks, and how fast it returns

| System | Loss over 22 weeks of no running | Return time on retraining |
|---|---|---|
| **Plasma volume** | 5-12%, lost within the first 1-3 weeks of detraining (Coyle et al. 1984) | Days to 2 weeks. This is why HR is elevated at any given pace in weeks 1-2, and why it drops sharply around week 3 for no apparent reason |
| **Stroke volume / cardiac output** | Reduced, tracking plasma volume initially, then structural cardiac remodelling over months | 2-6 weeks for the volume-driven component |
| **VO₂max** | Falls ~6-8% in the first 3 weeks, then more slowly to a plateau; maintenance cycling will have blunted this considerably (Coyle et al. 1984, 1985; Mujika & Padilla 2000) | 6-12 weeks to near-baseline in a previously trained athlete |
| **Mitochondrial density and oxidative enzymes** | Substantial decline over weeks-months; running-specific musculature loses more than the cycling-trained musculature | 2-4 months of consistent volume |
| **Capillary density** | Declines slowly over months; partially preserved by any aerobic work | Months |
| **Muscle glycogen storage and fat oxidation at a given intensity** | Reduced | 4-8 weeks |
| **Running economy** | Largely preserved; likely improved by the strength block | Weeks — needs running exposure to express |
| **Tendon stiffness / load tolerance** | Running-specific tendon loading tolerance substantially reduced. Tendon collagen turnover in the core is extremely slow (Heinemeier et al. 2013) | **3-6 months.** Slowest of everything here |
| **Bone (tibia, metatarsals, femoral neck)** | Reduced running-specific bone loading stimulus; remodelling takes months and passes through a transient *weakened* phase early in re-loading | **3-6+ months**, and the first 4-8 weeks are the vulnerable window |

The lifting block will have preserved more than a pure layoff would — tendon and bone respond
to heavy resistance loading, and the maintenance cycling and rucking preserved aerobic and (in
the case of rucking) some impact-adjacent loading. But **resistance-training tendon adaptation
is not interchangeable with running tendon adaptation.** The Achilles under a heavy calf raise
and the Achilles at 2.8 m/s with a 40 ms ground contact are loaded at different rates, in
different ranges, for different numbers of cycles.

### The mismatch, which is the whole point of this section

**Cardiovascular fitness returns faster than musculoskeletal tolerance, and that mismatch is
the single biggest injury risk on a return to running.**

Concretely: by week 3-4, plasma volume has re-expanded, VO₂max is recovering, economy is
intact, and running at a decent pace will feel comfortable. Aerobically the athlete is perhaps
80% back. The tendons and bones, at that same moment, are perhaps 30-40% back, and — this is
the dangerous part — they produce no signal to say so. There is no breathlessness from an
under-remodelled tibia. The athlete's own perception of effort is calibrated to the system that
has recovered, and it will report "this is easy, I should do more" while the system that has
not recovered accumulates microdamage.

This is why the first mesocycle in section 6 is governed by a calendar and a symptom log rather
than by how the running feels, and it is the one place in this whole file where the athlete's
subjective report should be overruled.

Three rules follow:

1. **The first four weeks are non-negotiable in duration.** Feeling good is not a criterion for
   shortening them. Feeling bad is a criterion for extending them.
2. **Bone stress injury has a warning sign and it is not soreness.** Focal, point-tender pain
   over bone that worsens through a run rather than warming up, and hurts on hopping, stops
   running that day and gets assessed. Diffuse muscular soreness that eases within 10 minutes
   does not.
3. **The proximal hamstring gate governs speed, not volume.** Volume can progress on the
   tissue-build schedule regardless. Anything above Zone 3 — strides, intervals, fast downhills
   — waits for the return-to-running criteria in
   `strength-conditioning-coach/references/injury-guardrails.md` §1 to be met and reported, and
   even then enters as 4-6 × 8-10 s **uphill** strides, not as a session and not on the flat.

## 9. Measuring progress

### What to track

| Metric | How | What it tells you | Cadence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Resting HR** | On waking, same conditions, same position | Trend, not absolute value. A sustained rise of 5+ bpm over ~5 days indicates accumulated fatigue or illness before performance drops | Daily, read weekly |
| **HR at a fixed easy pace** | Same route, same pace, same conditions | The cleanest available field measure of aerobic fitness. Falling HR at the same pace over weeks is the base building | Weekly, one designated route |
| **Pace at a fixed easy HR** | The inverse; useful when pace is variable | Same signal, read the other way. Use whichever the athlete finds easier to hold constant | Weekly |
| **Aerobic decoupling (Pw:HR / Pa:HR)** | Over a steady aerobic run of 60+ min, compare pace-to-HR ratio in the first half against the second. Under ~5% drift indicates aerobic durability at that intensity (Allen & Coggan) | The best single indicator of whether the *base* is built, as opposed to the top end. High decoupling at easy pace means more Zone 1 volume, not more intensity | Monthly, on a long run |
| **Weekly volume and the 3-week rolling average** | Simple log | The primary load-management number, and the one that predicts injury risk. Watch the *ratio* of this week to the trailing average more than the absolute figure (Gabbett 2016, with the caveat that acute:chronic ratios are contested and are a prompt to look, not a verdict) | Weekly |
| **Time trial or tune-up race** | 5K or a 20-30 min TT on a repeatable course | The only unambiguous measure of performance, and the anchor for re-setting zones | Every 6-8 weeks, never in the first 6 weeks back |
| **Symptom log** | Hamstring, knee, shin: 0-10, during and next morning | The governing signal of the first mesocycle. Same rule as the lifting block: ≤3/10 during, settled by the next morning, is acceptable | Every session |

### What to ignore

- **Day-to-day GPS pace.** GPS error under tree cover, in cities, and on trails is routinely
  2-5% — larger than most week-to-week fitness changes. Comparing today's easy pace to last
  Tuesday's is reading noise. Compare monthly, on the same route, or use HR-at-pace instead.
- **Third-party "fitness scores", race-time predictors, and recovery percentages.** They are
  opaque, proprietary, mostly HR-derived, and calibrated on populations rather than on this
  athlete. A fitness score that rises during a taper (because load falls) or falls during a
  base phase (because intensity falls) is describing the algorithm, not the athlete. **Never
  let one of these override a symptom report or a planned progression.**
- **Single-session HRV readings.** The 7-day rolling mean carries signal; one morning's number
  carries mostly measurement noise.
- **Elevation gain from barometric or GPS altimeters**, compared across devices. Useful within
  one device over time; meaningless between devices.
- **Comparisons to the pre-layoff self.** Paces from last August are not a target for March.
  The only useful comparison for the first 12 weeks is against week 1 of this build.

The reporting habit that matters: when the athlete asks "am I getting fitter", answer with
HR-at-fixed-pace trend, decoupling, and completed volume — three numbers with mechanisms behind
them — rather than with whatever the watch summarised.

## 10. Evidence notes

- **Joyner (1991)**, *Med Sci Sports Exerc*, and **Joyner & Coyle (2008)**, *J Physiol*,
  "Endurance exercise performance: the physiology of champions" — the VO₂max × fractional
  utilisation ÷ running economy model that structures section 1.
- **Seiler & Kjerland (2006)**, *Scand J Med Sci Sports* — elite endurance athletes distribute
  roughly 75-80% of sessions below the first ventilatory threshold; the original descriptive
  observation behind "80/20".
- **Seiler (2010)**, *Int J Sports Physiol Perform* — review of intensity and duration
  distribution; the source usually cited for the polarised model, and explicitly framed as
  best-practice observation rather than controlled comparison.
- **Esteve-Lanao et al. (2007)**, *J Strength Cond Res* — sub-elite runners at ~80/20 improved
  more over 5 months than a more threshold-weighted group.
- **Stöggl & Sperlich (2014)**, *Front Physiol* — 9-week comparison in which polarised training
  produced greater VO₂max and performance gains than threshold, high-volume, or HIIT-only
  blocks.
- **Casado et al. (2021)**, *J Strength Cond Res* — analysis of world-class distance runners
  associating performance with high volumes of easy running plus deliberate short-interval and
  tempo work; supports pyramidal distributions being common at the elite level.
- **Buist et al. (2008)**, *Am J Sports Med* — randomised trial in novice runners; a graded
  13-week programme produced **no reduction in running-related injuries** versus a standard
  8-week programme. The central evidence against treating the 10% rule as protective.
- **Nielsen et al. (2014)**, *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* — weekly running-distance progressions
  above roughly 30% were associated with distance-related injuries; the basis for the
  load-spike framing used for the long run and the weekly step size.
- **Gabbett (2016)**, *Br J Sports Med* — the training-injury prevention paradox: high chronic
  loads are protective, rapid spikes relative to chronic load are not. Subsequent
  methodological critiques of acute:chronic ratios mean this is a prompt to investigate, not a
  threshold to obey.
- **Wilson et al. (2012)**, *J Strength Cond Res* — concurrent-training meta-analysis;
  interference scales with modality (running > cycling), duration, frequency, and intensity.
- **Rønnestad & Mujika (2014)**, *Scand J Med Sci Sports* — review: heavy strength training
  improves endurance performance primarily via running/cycling economy, without increasing
  VO₂max.
- **Balsalobre-Fernández et al. (2016)**, *J Strength Cond Res* — meta-analysis: strength
  training improves running economy in middle- and long-distance runners, with heavy and
  plyometric loading most effective.
- **Blagrove, Howatson & Hayes (2018)**, *Sports Med* — systematic review of strength training
  in middle- and long-distance runners; consistent economy and performance benefit, no
  hypertrophy penalty at the volumes used.
- **Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen (2014)**, *Br J Sports Med* — meta-analysis: strength
  training almost halved overuse injuries and reduced all sports injuries to under a third;
  stretching showed no protective effect.
- **Robineau et al. (2016)**, *J Strength Cond Res* — concurrent sessions separated by longer
  recovery preserved strength adaptation better than back-to-back sessions; the basis for the
  ≥6 h separation rule.
- **Coyle et al. (1984, 1985)**, *J Appl Physiol* — detraining time course in trained endurance
  athletes: rapid early VO₂max and stroke-volume decline driven largely by plasma volume loss,
  then a slower plateau; oxidative enzyme activity declines on a separate, longer timescale.
- **Mujika & Padilla (2000, 2001)**, *Sports Med* — two-part review of short- and long-term
  detraining; the source for the staged loss ordering in section 8.
- **Heinemeier et al. (2013)**, *FASEB J* — carbon-14 dating of human Achilles tendon showed
  essentially no turnover in the tendon core after early adulthood, indicating that tendon
  adaptation is slow and largely peripheral. Mechanistic support for tissue tolerance being the
  slowest-returning quality.
- **Jones & Vanhatalo (2017)**, *Sports Med* — the critical power/critical speed concept as a
  field estimate of the maximal metabolic steady state; the basis for the two-effort zone
  anchor.
- **Friel** — the 30-minute time trial protocol in which the average HR of the final 20 minutes
  approximates lactate threshold heart rate. A widely used field convention rather than a
  validated laboratory equivalence; treat the resulting LTHR as an estimate with a few beats of
  error.
- **Midgley, McNaughton & Jones (2007)**, *Sports Med* — review of training to enhance VO₂max
  in well-trained runners; the small remaining headroom in trained athletes and the intensity
  required to access it.
- **Allen & Coggan** — the Pw:HR / Pa:HR decoupling convention (drift under ~5% over a steady
  aerobic effort as an indicator of aerobic durability). A practitioner metric with sound
  physiological rationale rather than a validated laboratory measure.
- **Talk test** — work by Foster and colleagues established that the point at which comfortable
  speech breaks down corresponds closely with the ventilatory thresholds, making it a valid and
  equipment-free anchor for the Zone 1/Zone 2 boundary.
