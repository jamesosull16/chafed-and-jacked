# Return to Running

The January return is the single highest-risk moment in the whole year. This file owns it: the
gate that decides whether week 1 happens at all, the twelve-week build, the order stressors come
back in, and the rules that catch a problem while it is still a niggle. Everything here is a
constraint on the run programme, not a suggestion. When the athlete wants to move faster than
this file allows, say so plainly and give the reason — the reason is always tissue, never
fitness.

Active flags for this athlete: `highHamstring`, `knee`, `tightHips`, `ankleMobility`.

## 1. The core problem, stated openly

Twenty-two weeks of lifting with 2 × 20-30 min of easy non-impact cardio per week leaves the
athlete in a specific and dangerous state: **the cardiovascular system is nearly ready and the
tissues are not.**

The two systems recover on completely different clocks.

| System | Time to return | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Plasma volume, stroke volume | 1-3 weeks | Fast, fluid- and cardiac-driven; the maintenance cardio held most of it (Mujika & Padilla 2000) |
| Mitochondrial density, capillarisation | 4-8 weeks | Slower to lose, slower to rebuild, but rebuilds on a training stimulus measured in weeks |
| Tendon (Achilles, proximal hamstring) | 8-12+ weeks | Collagen turnover is slow; tendon stiffness and cross-sectional adaptation lag loading badly |
| Bone (tibia, metatarsal, femoral neck) | 3-6 months | A remodelling cycle runs months, and the early phase transiently *weakens* the site |
| Plantar fascia, joint capsule | 8-12 weeks | Low vascularity, slow adaptation, no symptoms until it fails |

So by week 3 the athlete will be able to hold a conversational pace for 40 minutes without
breathing hard, and their tibia will have had three weeks of impact after five months of none.
The felt limiter — breathing, legs burning — has moved out of the way, and the actual limiter is
silent right up until it isn't. **The mismatch between aerobic readiness and tissue tolerance is
what causes the injury, not lack of fitness.** Every rule in this file exists to hold the run
volume down to what the slow tissues can absorb while the fast systems shout that there's more
available.

### Why being stronger makes it worse

The instinct is that arriving at January stronger is protective. Long term it is — strength
training reduces overuse injury rates substantially (Lauersen et al. 2014), and the hamstring
work in particular is the best insurance available for this athlete's main flag.

Short term it is a liability, for one reason: **strength removes the sensation that would
otherwise cap the session.** Five months of lower-body hypertrophy work means the quads, glutes,
and calves can produce and absorb force for far longer than the tendons and bone can tolerate
the resulting cycles. Muscle is the system that tells you to stop. It has been upgraded. The
systems that can't tell you anything have not.

Concretely: the athlete will finish week 2's walk-run feeling insulted by it. That is the plan
working correctly, not evidence that it's wrong. The failure mode is the week-3 or week-4 "I felt
great so I just kept going" run — one session at 2-3× the prescribed duration, landing as a load
spike into structures with no capacity buffer. It rarely hurts that day. It shows up as focal
shin pain around week 6.

## 2. Clearance criteria — the gate before week 1

**Nobody starts week 1 until every criterion below is met and has been actually tested.** Not
assumed, not inferred from the absence of complaints. Test them in the last week of the lifting
block and report the results explicitly.

Ownership, so neither skill makes the other's call:

- `strength-conditioning-coach` **owns and reports** the proximal hamstring loading criteria
  (items H1-H5 below). It runs the four-stage protocol in `injury-guardrails.md` §1 and states
  which stage the hamstring is at.
- `endurance-running-coach` **owns the return-to-run decision** and everything after it. It asks
  for the hamstring report, applies the running-readiness screens, and makes the call.

If the hamstring report is unavailable, the gate is not met. Do not start on the assumption that
it probably is.

### Hamstring gate (reported by `strength-conditioning-coach`)

| # | Criterion | Why it's on the list |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | Stage 4 loading tolerated symptom-free for **2+ consecutive weeks** — deficit RDL, good morning, or full-range seated leg curl at working loads | Stage 4 is lengthened-position loading, which is the closest lifting analogue to what running does to the proximal tendon. Tolerating it is the whole point of the block |
| H2 | Single-leg strength symmetry within **~10% L/R** (single-leg bridge or leg curl) | A limb deficit at the point of return is a well-established reinjury risk factor, and running is 100% single-leg work |
| H3 | **No pain on prolonged sitting**, no morning stiffness at the ischial tuberosity | The classic proximal tendinopathy presentation. Present at rest means the tendon is already irritable before any running load is added |
| H4 | Eccentric capacity — Nordics or slow single-leg RDL eccentrics at full range without provocation | Terminal swing in running is an eccentric contraction at near-peak hamstring length. This is the specific quality being tested |
| H5 | **4 consecutive weeks** with no working pain above 2/10 in any hinge or curl pattern | Note this is stricter than the block's in-training rule of ≤3/10. The bar rises at the point of return because running removes the ability to modulate load mid-rep |

If any of H1-H5 is unmet, name the specific one and what would close it. Do not soften the
report because the calendar says January.

### Running-readiness screens (owned here)

These are separate from the hamstring and get tested independently. All are pass/fail.

| # | Screen | Target | Why this specific test |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | **Single-leg calf raise capacity** | **≥25 full-range reps each side**, controlled tempo, knee straight, no hip or knee cheating | The calf-Achilles complex absorbs the largest single share of the load in running, and it is the most commonly under-prepared structure at the point of return. Normative work puts healthy adults in the low-to-mid twenties (Hébert-Losier et al. 2017), so ≥25 is a defensible bar rather than an aspirational one. Fail this and the Achilles becomes the limiter by week 5 |
| R2 | Single-leg bridge hold | 30 s each side, no cramping, no ischial pain, pelvis level | Posterior chain endurance under a hip-neutral load. Cheap, and it re-checks H3 from a different angle |
| R3 | Pain-free hopping | 2 × 30 s single-leg hopping, each side, pain-free during and next morning | The first genuine impact test. If 60 hops provoke something, 3,000 foot strikes will |
| R4 | Brisk walking | 30 minutes continuous, pain-free, no next-day symptoms | The floor. If sustained low-grade loading provokes symptoms, running is not a question yet |
| R5 | Ankle dorsiflexion | Knee-to-wall within 2 cm side-to-side, and at least at the block's most recent measured value | Asymmetric dorsiflexion changes foot strike and loading distribution. The block has been measuring this every two weeks — use that number |
| R6 | No knee pain on stairs or descents | Symptom-free descending a full flight | Anterior knee pain on descent is the early signal that the knee flag will resurface once downhills enter (§5) |

### If the gate isn't met

Do not delay the whole return. Hold the run start and keep the specific limiter progressing:

- **R1 failed** — 3-4 weeks of dedicated heavy slow calf loading (§7) before week 1. This is the
  most common single failure and the easiest to fix.
- **H1 or H4 failed** — hand back to `strength-conditioning-coach` for continued stage 3-4 work.
  Running does not fix this and will make it worse.
- **R3 or R4 failed** — walk-and-hop progression only, retest weekly.

Meanwhile keep conditioning at the transition dose (3-4 × 30-40 min) so aerobic base isn't lost
while the gate is being closed.

## 3. Weeks 1-4 — the walk-run build

### The rules that govern this phase

- **Run entirely by time, never by distance.** Distance targets invite pace to creep, and pace is
  the variable that turns a tolerable run into an intolerable one. Minutes are the load unit here
  and stay the load unit through week 12.
- **All of it easy — conversational, full sentences, no exceptions.** There is no session in the
  first four weeks where breathing hard is correct.
- **Every run on flat, forgiving surfaces.** Tarmac, path, treadmill, grass. No trail, no camber,
  no hills, no track.
- **Minimum 48 hours between run sessions for the first fortnight.** Bone and tendon adaptation
  happens in the gap, not in the session, and the early remodelling phase needs the time.
- **The next day is the test, not the session.** Judge every run by how the athlete feels the
  following morning — first ten steps out of bed, and whether anything is stiff, focal, or new.
  A run that felt fine and produces a sore spot at 7am the next day was too much.
- Walk portions are genuine walking, not jogging. The point of the walk is unloading.
- Every session gets 5 minutes of brisk walking before and after. That is not padding; it is the
  tissue warming and the cool-down that lets the athlete assess how things actually feel.

### The build

| Wk | Runs | Session structure | Run min/session | Weekly run min | ≈ km |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 8 × [1 min run / 2 min walk] | 8 | 24 | ~4 |
| 2 | 3 | 6 × [2 min run / 2 min walk] | 12 | 36 | ~6 |
| 3 | 3 | 4 × [4 min run / 2 min walk] | 16 | 48 | ~8 |
| 4 | 4 | 4 × [4 min run / 2 min walk] | 16 | 64 | ~10 |

Kilometres are shown as an approximation only, at a deliberately slow ~6:15/km. They are an
output, not a target. Do not put them in front of the athlete as the number that matters.

Note what changes and when. Weeks 1-3 hold frequency at 3 and grow the run interval. Week 4
holds the interval at 4 minutes and adds a fourth session instead — **one variable at a time**,
which is the same rule that governs §5. It's tempting to lengthen the interval *and* add the
session, because week 4 feels easy by then. Don't; the point of the structure is that when
something flares you know which change caused it.

### Progression gates

Advance only if all three hold. If any fails, repeat the week — repeating a week costs seven
days, a bone stress injury costs eight to twelve weeks.

| Gate | Criterion |
|---|---|
| Next-morning | No new focal pain, no morning stiffness beyond generalised muscular soreness, first steps out of bed normal |
| Within-session | Every run interval completed at conversational effort, no limping, no compensation |
| Hamstring | No deep buttock pain, no pain on sitting, no ischial stiffness at any point in the week |

### Why the percentage increase looks alarming and isn't

Week 1 to week 2 is +50% in run minutes. Applied at week 10 that would be reckless. Here the
absolute increment is 12 minutes of running spread over three sessions. **Percentage rules break
down at low absolute volumes** — judge the early weeks on absolute increments (aim for +10 to
+16 run minutes per week across the whole week; week 4's +16 comes entirely from adding a fourth
session, not from lengthening any run, which is the cheaper way to buy the increment) and switch to percentage-based reasoning from
about week 6, once there is a meaningful base to take a percentage of. This is the same reason
the acute:chronic ratio is uninterpretable before week 6 (§6).

## 4. Weeks 5-12 — continuous running and volume build

### The build

| Wk | Runs | Session structure | Weekly run min | ≈ km | What's new |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 4 | 3 × [6 × (3 run / 1 walk)], 1 × [7 × (3 run / 1 walk)] | 75 | ~12 | Walk breaks shorten to 1 min |
| 6 | 4 | 3 × [4 × (6 run / 1 walk)], 1 × [2 × (9 run / 1 walk)] | 90 | ~15 | Longest single run block reaches 9 min |
| 7 | 4 | 25, 25, 25, 30 — all **continuous** | 105 | ~17 | First fully continuous running |
| 8 | 4 | 20, 20, 20, 25 continuous | 85 | ~14 | **Down week + gate** (see below) |
| 9 | 4 | 25 + 4 uphill strides, 30, 30, **40 long** | 125 | ~21 | Uphill strides; long run designated |
| 10 | 5 | 25 + 6 uphill strides, 25, 30, 25, **45 long** | 150 | ~26 | Fifth session |
| 11 | 4 | 25 + 6 uphill strides incl. **2 × 5 min threshold**, 25, 30, **50 long** | 130 | ~23 | Down week, first light quality |
| 12 | 5 | 30 incl. 2 × 8 min threshold, 30 + 4 **flat strides**, 25, 30, **70 long** | 185 | ~33 | Volume peak; flat strides |

Structure of the block: build 5-6-7, absorb at 8; build 9-10, introduce quality on the reduced
volume of 11, then the volume peak at 12. **New intensity always enters on a reduced-volume
week.** That is why the first threshold session sits in week 11 rather than week 12 — adding
volume and intensity in the same week is the most reliable way to produce a setback.

**Strides run uphill for three weeks before going flat.** Weeks 9, 10 and 11 are uphill only —
8-10 s on a 6-8% gradient, walking down. Flat strides (15-20 s) enter at week 12, once three
clean uphill sessions have banked. The gradient shortens the stride and cuts peak hamstring
length at the moment force peaks, so uphill is the cheapest possible reintroduction of velocity
for this athlete's flag.

**Why weeks 8 and 11 aren't full 60-70% down weeks.** They land at ~81% and ~87% of the week
before, above the 60-70% rule this file otherwise applies. That's deliberate at this stage of a
return: the chronic base is still climbing steeply from near zero, and a full 60-70% cut would
drag the 28-day average down far enough that the *following* build week becomes a bigger relative
spike than the week it was meant to protect against. From week 13, once the base is established,
the down weeks go to the full 60-70%.

The week 8 gate is a real decision point, not a formality. Before proceeding to week 9, confirm:
no symptom has recurred in weeks 5-7, the Achilles is quiet in the mornings, the calf raise
number from R1 has held or improved, and the hamstring has stayed silent through four weeks of
continuous impact. If any is shaky, repeat weeks 7-8 rather than advancing.

### Session definitions

- **Easy** — conversational, roughly 60-70% HRmax, RPE 3-4/10. Use `HRmax ≈ 208 − 0.7 × age`
  (Tanaka et al. 2001) if no measured value exists. If in doubt, slower.
- **Long run** — same easy effort, just longer. No pace target, no progression, no finishing
  fast. The long run is a duration stimulus for tendon, bone, and fat oxidation, and turning it
  into a workout wastes the stimulus and adds the risk.
- **Strides, uphill (week 9)** — 8-10 s at controlled fast on a 6-8% gradient, full walk-down
  recovery. Uphill first, always: running up a gradient shortens the stride and reduces peak
  hamstring length at the moment force peaks, which is the exact thing being reintroduced
  cautiously.
- **Strides, flat (week 10)** — 15-20 s at controlled fast (not sprint, roughly 5k-to-mile
  effort), full walk recovery, mid-run or at the end of an easy run. Only after three clean
  uphill stride sessions. **Never on a downhill.**
- **Threshold** — comfortably hard, sustainable-for-an-hour effort, RPE 6-7/10, able to speak in
  short phrases. Broken into blocks with 2 min easy jogging between. Not a time trial.

### The long run as a fraction of the week

Conventional guidance caps the long run at roughly a third of weekly volume. Week 12's 70-minute
long run is 38% of 185 — above that, deliberately, because long-run duration is the specific
adaptation being chased at that point and weekly volume is still low in absolute terms. The
trade is stated so it can be monitored: **if anything niggles in weeks 11-12, the long run is the
first thing trimmed**, not the easy runs, because it is the session carrying the disproportionate
load.

### Kilometres will rise faster than minutes — watch for it

By week 10-12, easy pace will have come back substantially (an experienced runner's pace returns
long before their tissue tolerance does). The same 30-minute run covers meaningfully more ground
than it did in week 5. That is a real increase in impact cycles and in ground reaction force,
and it does not appear anywhere in the minutes column.

Two consequences. First, keep prescribing minutes — that way pace improvement can't quietly
inflate the plan. Second, if the athlete's average easy pace drops by more than ~20 s/km inside
a fortnight, treat that as a load increase in its own right and hold volume flat that week.

**One carve-out.** Treadmill grade intervals (5-6 × 3 min at 6-8%, easy jog recovery) may enter
at **week 13**, ahead of outdoor hills at week 13-14 rather than behind them, because the entire
risk of hills at this stage is the descent and a treadmill has no descent. Not week 11 — week 11
is a reduced-volume week whose one new stressor is threshold, and the no-new-stressor-on-a-down-week
rule outranks the convenience. Cap the effort at threshold, not harder.

### What is deliberately NOT introduced before week 12

| Stressor | Not before | Why, for this athlete specifically |
|---|---|---|
| **True speed work** (intervals at 5k pace or faster, sprinting) | Week 16+ | Hamstring musculotendon length peaks in terminal swing, and peak length and peak force coincide at high speed — the exact lengthened-under-load position the entire five-month block was built to protect (Chumanov et al. 2011). Threshold running at week 11-12 is well below the speed at which this becomes a problem; 5k pace and faster is not |
| **Downhill running** | Week 14+ | Downhills add eccentric loading and a higher impact peak (Gottschall & Kram 2005), loading the hamstring in a lengthened position and the patellofemoral joint on descent. Two active flags, one stressor |
| **Hills (uphill)** | Week 13-14 | Lower risk than downhills — uphill running is more concentric and reduces impact — but it lengthens the hip flexors and loads the calf/Achilles hard, and the Achilles is the structure with the thinnest margin at this point. Also: going up means coming down |
| **Trail / uneven ground** | Week 14+ | Unpredictable foot placement, camber, and ankle range demands. The `ankleMobility` flag makes this worse than it would be for a typical runner |
| **Track work** | Week 16+ | Repeated tight left-hand bends load the two limbs asymmetrically, and bend running increases hamstring demand |
| **Doubles / back-to-back run days beyond one pair** | Week 12+ | Recovery between impact sessions is where bone remodelling happens. Consecutive run days can enter cautiously at week 10-12 for the easiest two sessions, not before |

The one that gets argued about is hills. The athlete will point out that hill running is
protective in the long run and that uphill work is easier on the joints than flat running. Both
are true. The answer is that the constraint isn't the hill, it's the descent that follows it and
the Achilles load on the way up, and that the schedule moves it back by four weeks, not deletes
it.

## 5. The reintroduction order, and why it's in that order

Every stressor that was removed comes back, in this sequence. The order is set by how much each
one loads the two flagged structures relative to the adaptation it buys.

| # | Stressor | Earliest | Why here in the order |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Easy volume** | Week 1 | The base everything else sits on. It's the only stimulus that drives bone and tendon adaptation, and it's the lowest-intensity way to get it |
| 2 | **Frequency** | Week 4 | Adding a session distributes the same weekly load into smaller doses. That's lower peak load per session for more total adaptation — the cheapest progression available |
| 3 | **Long run** | Week 9 | Duration at low intensity. The load is cumulative rather than peak, which is what tendon and bone respond to, but it needs a base of continuous running to sit on |
| 4 | **Strides — uphill wk 9, flat wk 10** | Week 9 | The first exposure to higher-velocity mechanics, in doses short enough (8-20 s) that fatigue never accumulates. Uphill first: the gradient shortens the stride and cuts peak hamstring length at the moment force peaks. Flat strides follow after three clean uphill sessions |
| 5 | **Hills — uphill only** | Week 13-14 | Concentric-dominant, lower impact than flat running, big calf and glute stimulus. Walk the descents at first, genuinely |
| 6 | **Tempo / threshold** | Week 11-12 | Sustained moderate intensity, but well below the speed at which hamstring stretch becomes the limiting factor. Sits before hills in time but after them in risk order — it can come early precisely because it's sub-maximal |
| 7 | **Downhill running** | Week 14 | Eccentric loading, elevated impact peak, and the proximal hamstring in a lengthened position. Enter with short, shallow descents at easy effort |
| 8 | **Intervals / true speed** | Week 16+ | Maximal hamstring lengthening under maximal force. Last, and only after 7 has been tolerated |

**Downhill running and true speed work go last, and together they are the highest-risk
combination in the whole return for this athlete's proximal hamstring.** Fast running downhill —
a hard finish on a descent, chasing someone down a hill, a downhill parkrun — is the single
scenario most likely to end the build. Name it explicitly to the athlete rather than leaving it
as an inference from the table.

### The one-new-stressor rule

**Introduce one new stressor at a time, and hold it for at least two weeks before adding
another.** Two reasons, and the second matters more than the first:

1. Two simultaneous stressors produce a larger load increase than either was assessed for.
2. If something goes wrong, you cannot tell which one caused it — so you either remove both
   (losing an adaptation you didn't need to lose) or guess wrong and repeat the injury.

"Held for two weeks" means two weeks in which the stressor was present and produced no symptom
carry-over, not two weeks on the calendar. Volume increases count as a stressor here too — which
is why week 11 drops volume: threshold entering is the new thing that week, so nothing else moves.

## 6. Load monitoring that actually works

### Session RPE × duration

The simplest internal-load metric worth using (Foster et al. 2001): rate the whole session 1-10
about 20-30 minutes after finishing, multiply by duration in minutes.

```
Session load (AU) = sRPE (1-10) × duration (min)
```

A 45-minute easy run at RPE 3 is 135 AU. A 30-minute threshold session at RPE 7 is 210 AU. That
comparison is the entire value of the metric: it prices intensity and duration on one scale, so
a week where volume dropped but intensity rose doesn't read as a rest week.

Two rules for it to mean anything. Rate the session **after** the endorphins settle, not while
finishing. And rate the whole session, not the hardest interval in it.

Include the lifting sessions in the weekly total. Two lifting sessions at RPE 7 × 60 min is
840 AU, which is not a rounding error against a 600-900 AU run week. A running build that ignores
the strength work in its load accounting will systematically under-count in exactly the weeks
the lifting is going well.

### The acute:chronic workload ratio — what it claims and what it's worth

**The claim.** Divide this week's load (acute, 7-day) by the rolling 28-day average (chronic).
Ratios much above ~1.5 have been associated with elevated injury risk in team sports, with a
"sweet spot" around 0.8-1.3 (Gabbett 2016, Hulin et al. 2016).

**The honest caveats**, because this gets over-sold:

- The ratio is mathematically coupled — the acute week is *inside* the chronic average, which
  creates spurious associations independent of any real effect (Lolli et al. 2019).
- The conceptual and statistical foundations have been substantively criticised, including the
  original "sweet spot" thresholds and the discretisation of a continuous variable into bins
  (Impellizzeri et al. 2020).
- It was developed in team sports with different load profiles, and has never been validated as
  a decision rule for an individual runner returning from a lay-off.
- It is uninterpretable in the first ~6 weeks here, because a 28-day average that includes weeks
  of near-zero running isn't a chronic load in any meaningful sense. Weeks 1-5 of this plan
  produce ratios of 1.3-1.4 that mean nothing at all.

**The usable version.** Treat it as a monitoring aid that flags weeks worth a second look, never
as a law that overrides how the athlete actually feels:

| ACWR (from week 6 onward) | Read as |
|---|---|
| < 0.8 | Detraining or an unplanned drop. Fine for a deliberate down week, worth asking about otherwise |
| 1.0 - 1.3 | The working range. Progressing at a rate the tissue plausibly tolerates |
| 1.3 - 1.5 | Watch. Acceptable once, not two weeks running, and not with a new stressor in the same week |
| > 1.5 | Warning. Something jumped. Check whether it was planned, and hold volume flat next week |

### The practical implementation: 3 weeks up, 1 week down

Nobody wants to compute a rolling average every Sunday. The 3:1 structure is the version that
survives contact with reality — three progressive weeks, then a fourth at roughly 60-70% of the
third, matching the down-week rule in `running-injury-guardrails.md` §8. Applied correctly it keeps the ratio inside 1.0-1.3 automatically, because the down week
resets the chronic average downward just enough to make the following build week arithmetically
sane.

In this plan: weeks 5-7 build, 8 absorbs; 9-10 build, 11 absorbs (and takes on the new intensity
instead), 12 peaks.

The down week is not optional and is not "a week off". It is the week the accumulated stimulus
becomes adaptation. Skipping it because the previous three weeks went well is the most common way
a build unravels, and it unravels two to three weeks later, not immediately.

### What to log per run

Five fields. Any more and it stops getting filled in.

| Field | Why |
|---|---|
| Duration (min) and structure | The load unit. Structure so a walk-run week isn't logged as continuous |
| sRPE 1-10 | Internal load. The intensity half of the metric |
| Surface and terrain | The most common unlogged confound — a "normal 30 min easy" on trail is a different session |
| Next-morning check: any focal pain? where? 0-10? | The actual test (§3). Log it the morning after, not at the end of the run |
| One-line note | Sleep, stress, shoes, "felt heavy" — this is what makes a pattern visible in retrospect |

Review these weekly. The signal to look for is not a single bad number; it is the same location
appearing in the next-morning field twice in a fortnight.

## 7. Tissue-specific preparation that runs alongside

The run build does not carry the whole load of preparing the tissue. Two lifting sessions per
week continue through the build (`program-design.md` §7), and their content is not arbitrary —
each element below is there to close a specific gap that running alone won't close.

### Calf and Achilles

The highest-yield work in this section, because R1 is the most commonly failed screen and the
Achilles is the structure with the least margin at the point of return.

| Element | Prescription | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy slow calf raise, **straight knee** | 3-4 × 6-8, 3 s up / 3 s down, full range with a pause at the bottom | Loads gastrocnemius and the Achilles at length. Heavy slow resistance produces outcomes comparable to eccentric-only protocols with better adherence (Beyer et al. 2015) |
| Heavy slow calf raise, **bent knee** (seated) | 3 × 8-12, same tempo | Soleus takes a disproportionate share of running load and is missed entirely by straight-knee work |
| Progression to plyometric | From week 6-8 only: pogo hops, 3 × 20 s; later single-leg | Restores tendon stiffness and elastic energy return, which heavy slow work builds strength for but doesn't fully train |
| Bottom-position loaded stretch | 2 × 20 s at the bottom of a loaded raise | Doubles as ankle dorsiflexion work for the `ankleMobility` flag. Two targets, one movement |

Never introduce plyometrics in the same week as a volume increase or a new stressor. They count
under §5's one-at-a-time rule.

### Bone

This is the section that can't be shortcut, and it's worth explaining rather than asserting.

**Running is the bone stimulus.** No lifting exercise, no supplement, and no amount of cycling
produces the site-specific strain at the tibia and metatarsals that running does. Bone adapts to
the loading it actually receives, where it receives it — so bone tolerance cannot be built in
advance of the run build, only *by* it.

**And remodelling lags loading by weeks.** A remodelling cycle begins with osteoclastic
resorption, and resorption precedes formation. For a period of weeks after a new loading
stimulus the loaded site is transiently *more* porous than before. Adaptation follows, but late.

Those two facts together produce the defining feature of bone stress injury: **it appears 3-8
weeks into a build, not on the day of the overload.** The athlete who had a great week 3 and a
great week 4 gets shin pain in week 6 and cannot work out what they did wrong. They did it in
week 3. Training-load error is the dominant modifiable factor (Warden, Davis & Fredericson 2014).

Practical consequences:
- Weeks 5-8 are the highest-vigilance window for focal bone pain even though they feel easy.
- A clean week 4 is not evidence that week 5 can be aggressive. It is evidence about week 1.
- Adequate energy availability, calcium, and vitamin D are non-negotiable during the build.
  Hand the specifics to `sports-nutritionist` — under-fuelling a running build is a direct bone
  stress injury risk factor, and the athlete is coming off a lean-bulk surplus that will need
  revisiting as run volume climbs.

### Hamstring

Loading continues through the build, at stage 4 and unchanged in intent — this is not the moment
to declare it fixed. Eccentric knee-flexor work is the best-evidenced hamstring injury
prevention available (van Dyk et al. 2019), and stopping it because running has started throws
away the block's main asset.

| Element | Prescription | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Nordic curl or slow single-leg RDL eccentric | 2-3 × 5-8, 3-4 s eccentric, twice weekly | Eccentric strength at length, the quality tested by terminal swing |
| Single-leg RDL, full range | 3 × 8-10 per side | Symmetry maintenance — H2 is a gate criterion, not a one-off measurement |
| Hip thrust | 3 × 6-10 | Glute strength with the hip near neutral. Glute contribution reduces hamstring demand in late swing |
| Long-lever bridge isometric | Held in reserve | The first regression on a flare, and analgesic (`injury-guardrails.md` §1) |

Placement rule: keep heavy eccentric hamstring work at least 48 h away from the long run and from
any session containing strides or threshold. Two lengthened-load stimuli in 24 hours is the same
mistake as two new stressors in a week.

### Carry-over from the lifting block

| Keep | Add |
|---|---|
| Two lifting sessions/week at ~1/3 accumulation volume | Heavy slow calf raises, straight *and* bent knee |
| Hip thrust and single-leg RDL | Plyometric progression from week 6-8 |
| Nordic / slow eccentric hamstring work | Single-leg balance and hopping, ~5 min, for the ankle flag |
| The 6-8 min mobility block, every session, unchanged | Nothing else. The list is short on purpose |
| Knee-to-wall measurement every 2 weeks | |

The mobility block continues because **running consumes ankle dorsiflexion and hip extension; it
does not build them.** Twelve weeks of running with the mobility work dropped will leave the
athlete with less range than they started the build with, and reduced dorsiflexion changes how
load distributes at the knee and Achilles — which is how the `knee` flag comes back.

## 8. Warning signs during the build

Ask about these explicitly. Do not wait to be told — athletes reliably under-report during a
return, because reporting a symptom feels like it might cost them the build.

| Symptom | Interpretation | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Aches at the start of a run, eases within 10 min, doesn't return | Normal tissue warm-up response, common in tendon | **Continue.** Log it and watch the trend across the week |
| Pain that **worsens** as the run goes on | Load exceeding tolerance in real time. Different in kind from the above | **Stop the run** — walk home. Modify: drop to the previous week's volume, reassess in 48 h |
| Pain the morning after that wasn't there before the run | The primary early-warning signal (§3). Tissue didn't recover overnight | **Modify.** No running until a full pain-free morning. Then repeat the last completed week |
| **Focal bone tenderness** — pinpoint pain on a bone (shin, foot, femoral neck/groin) that you can cover with a fingertip, worse with impact, worse over successive days, aching at night | **Bone stress injury until proven otherwise.** The night ache and the day-on-day worsening are the discriminating features | **Stop running. Refer for imaging.** Do not trial-run it to see. A femoral neck or anterior tibial site is high-risk and can progress to fracture |
| Deep buttock pain, worse on sitting, ± radiating down the posterior thigh | Proximal hamstring tendinopathy recurrence — the block's known flag, presenting exactly as expected | **Stop running.** Revert to stage 1 isometrics and hand back to `strength-conditioning-coach`. Re-enter via §9 |
| Achilles stiffness in the first steps of the morning, easing within a few minutes | Early reactive tendon response. Very common at this stage, and manageable if caught | **Modify.** Hold volume flat, no new stressors, add isometric calf holds (5 × 45 s). If it worsens over a week or takes longer each morning to ease, stop running |
| Achilles pain that no longer warms up, or thickening/nodularity you can feel | Progressed beyond reactive | **Stop and refer** |
| Anterior knee pain on descents (stairs, downhills) | Patellofemoral load intolerance — the `knee` flag. Often driven by reduced dorsiflexion or a volume spike | **Modify.** Remove all descents, reduce volume 20-30%, reinforce quad isometrics (Spanish squat) and dorsiflexion work. Re-check knee-to-wall |
| Knee locking, giving way, or joint-line swelling | Not a load-management problem | **Stop and refer** |
| Numbness, tingling, or pins and needles anywhere in the leg | Neural. Load management will not fix it | **Stop and refer** |
| Sudden sharp pain with a "pop", or any acute onset mid-stride | Acute strain or worse | **Stop and refer** |
| Pain unrelated to loading, night pain, or pain that doesn't settle after two deloaded weeks | Non-mechanical or progressed | **Stop and refer** |

The line that matters: **"modify" means the run programme changes and the athlete keeps
training; "stop and refer" means the run programme is suspended and a clinician looks at it.**
Never blur the two to keep a build alive. Suspending a build for a fortnight to rule out a bone
stress injury costs a fortnight. Running through one costs a season, and femoral neck and
anterior tibial sites can progress to complete fracture.

## 9. Return to run after a setback during the build

Setbacks are expected. The plan is not invalidated by one; it is re-entered.

### Where to restart, by time missed

Judged from the last completed run session, and only once symptoms have settled.

**The missed-time table lives in `running-injury-guardrails.md` §8** — one table, so the two
files can't drift apart. Use it as written: 1-3 days resume where you left off, 4-7 days return
at ~80% of the last completed week, 1-2 weeks at ~70% with no intensity in the first week, 2-4
weeks at ~50-60% easy only, and beyond 4 weeks treat it as a fresh return.

One clause specific to this build: **beyond 4 weeks, restart the walk-run phase but compress
it.** An athlete who had eight clean weeks before the setback typically re-enters around week 3-4
of the plan within a fortnight, because the tissue adaptation from those eight weeks hasn't
entirely gone. Compress the ladder, don't skip it.

Those figures assume the underlying problem is resolved. If it isn't, none of it applies — the
symptom-based ladder below governs instead.

### The symptom-based re-entry ladder

Use this when a specific structure was the problem, regardless of how much time was missed. Each
rung requires **pain-free completion and a clean next morning** before advancing. One rung per
session, minimum 48 h between.

| Rung | Test | Advance when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 30 min brisk walking | Pain-free during and next morning |
| 2 | Single-leg calf raises and single-leg bridge, symptom-free at prior volumes | Screens R1 and R2 pass again |
| 3 | 2 × 30 s single-leg hopping, each side | Pain-free during and next morning (R3) |
| 4 | 6 × [1 min run / 2 min walk] | Pain-free during and next morning |
| 5 | 6 × [2 min run / 2 min walk] | As above |
| 6 | 4 × [4 min run / 2 min walk] | As above |
| 7 | 20 min continuous easy | As above — this is the re-entry point to the plan proper |

If the setback was the proximal hamstring, rungs 1-3 do not begin until
`strength-conditioning-coach` reports the hamstring back at stage 3-4 and the H-gate criteria met
again. The running skill does not re-clear the hamstring on its own.

### Rules that apply to every re-entry

- **Never restart at the volume you stopped at.** The tissue that failed is now less tolerant
  than it was, not more, and the layoff has reduced it further.
- **The stressor that caused it comes back last**, and only after two clean weeks at the volume
  where the problem appeared. If a downhill did it, the downhill is the last thing to return.
- **Two setbacks in the same structure inside eight weeks is not a programming problem.** It is
  a referral. Stop adjusting the plan and get it assessed.
- Keep non-impact conditioning going throughout, at whatever volume the symptom tolerates. The
  aerobic base is the one thing a setback doesn't have to cost.

## 10. What "success" looks like at week 12

Stated plainly so expectations are calibrated before week 1, not renegotiated in week 6.

| Marker | Week 12 |
|---|---|
| Run sessions | 4-5 per week |
| Weekly volume | ~185 min, roughly 30-35 km (up to 40 km if easy pace has returned to ~5:00/km) |
| Long run | 70-75 min continuous, easy throughout |
| Quality | Strides weekly — uphill from week 9, flat from week 12; one light threshold session (2 × 8 min) |
| Terrain | Flat and rolling; no sustained hills, no downhill running, no trail |
| Speed | Nothing at 5k pace or faster |
| Symptoms | None. No morning stiffness, no focal tenderness, no ischial pain, no sitting pain |
| Lifting | Two sessions per week maintained throughout, with calf and hamstring work intact |

A 90-minute long run and consistent 40+ km weeks are a week-15-to-16 marker, not a week-12 one.
The long run can't exceed roughly a third of weekly volume for long without becoming the session
that breaks things, so it advances as total volume advances, not ahead of it.

### The part the athlete won't like

**This is slower than you will want it to be, and slower than you will feel capable of.** By week
6 the aerobic system will be signalling that 60-minute runs are easy, and it will be right — they
would be easy, aerobically. The tendon and bone arguments in §1 and §7 are the entire reason the
plan does not do it.

And the specific thing to watch for: **being ahead of schedule is the single most reliable
predictor of a setback in this build.** Not fatigue, not soreness, not a bad session — those are
visible and get managed. Being ahead of schedule feels like success right up until week 6 or 8,
which is precisely the lag between a bone-loading error and its symptom. If the athlete reports
being two weeks ahead of the plan and feeling great, treat that as a warning and hold volume flat
for a week. Say why.

What actually counts as a successful twelve weeks: **twelve consecutive weeks completed, with no
week lost to symptoms.** That is the outcome that determines what weeks 13-24 can look like.
Volume at week 12 is almost irrelevant by comparison — an athlete arriving at week 12 uninjured
at 30 km can be at 60 km by week 24. An athlete who hit 45 km at week 10 and has a tibial stress
reaction is starting over in March.

## 11. Evidence notes

1. **Mujika & Padilla (2000)**, *Sports Medicine* — detraining time course. VO₂max and plasma
   volume fall within 2-4 weeks; mitochondrial and capillary adaptations are slower both to lose
   and to rebuild. The basis for §1's fast-versus-slow system split.
2. **Warden, Davis & Fredericson (2014)**, *JOSPT* — bone stress injury in runners. Training-load
   error is the dominant modifiable factor, and bone adaptation needs progressive, site-specific
   loading over months. Underpins §7's bone section and §8's red flag.
3. **Foster et al. (2001)**, *J Strength Cond Res* — session RPE as a practical internal-load
   measure; the sRPE × duration metric used in §6.
4. **Gabbett (2016)**, *Br J Sports Med*, with **Hulin et al. (2016)**, same journal — the
   training-injury prevention paradox and the original acute:chronic workload "sweet spot",
   including the >1.5 threshold cited in §6.
5. **Impellizzeri et al. (2020)**, *Int J Sports Physiol Perform*, with **Lolli et al. (2019)**,
   *Br J Sports Med* —
   conceptual pitfalls of the ACWR, and the mathematical coupling created by nesting the acute
   week inside the chronic average. The critique behind §6's caveats.
6. **Nielsen et al. (2014)**, *JOSPT* — large weekly increases in running distance (>30%)
   associated with distance-related running injuries; supports absolute-increment reasoning early
   and percentage reasoning later.
7. **Buist et al. (2008)**, *Am J Sports Med* — a graded 13-week novice programme did not reduce
   injury incidence versus a standard one. Why the "10% rule" is treated here as a heuristic.
8. **Beyer et al. (2015)**, *Am J Sports Med* — heavy slow resistance versus eccentric training
   for Achilles tendinopathy; comparable outcomes, better adherence for heavy slow resistance.
   The basis for §7's calf prescription.
9. **Hébert-Losier et al. (2017)**, *Physiotherapy* — normative values for the standing heel-rise
   test place healthy adults in the low-to-mid twenties at standardised cadence. Supports R1.
10. **Goom, Malliaras & Reiman (2016)**, *JOSPT* — proximal hamstring tendinopathy staged
    management; the four-stage progression and the tendon-compression mechanism the gate rests on.
11. **van Dyk et al. (2019)**, *Br J Sports Med* — meta-analysis; Nordic hamstring programmes
    substantially reduce hamstring injury rates. Why eccentric work continues through the build.
12. **Chumanov, Heiderscheit & Thelen (2011)**, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* — hamstring musculotendon
    dynamics in sprinting; peak length and high force coincide in terminal swing. Why speed is last.
13. **Gottschall & Kram (2005)**, *J Biomechanics* — downhill running increases the impact peak,
    supporting its placement near the end of the reintroduction order.
14. **Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen (2014)**, *Br J Sports Med* — strength training almost halved
    overuse injuries and reduced all sports injuries to under a third; stretching showed no
    protective effect. The long-term case for keeping the lifting.
15. **Tanaka, Monahan & Seals (2001)**, *J Am Coll Cardiol* — `HRmax ≈ 208 − 0.7 × age`, more
    accurate than `220 − age` in adults over 30.
