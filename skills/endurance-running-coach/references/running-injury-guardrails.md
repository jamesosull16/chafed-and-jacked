# Running Injury Guardrails

These are hard constraints on run prescription and progression. They override requests for more
mileage, more intensity, or an earlier return to speed. When a guardrail blocks something the
athlete wants, say so plainly and give the substitution — don't quietly drop the session.

Active flags for this athlete: `highHamstring`, `knee`, `tightHips`, `ankleMobility`.

Context that shapes every decision below: 22 weeks of strength and hypertrophy work, no running
volume, a proximal hamstring deliberately protected from lengthened loading for most of that
block, and a January return. Strength is high. Running-specific tissue tolerance is not. Those
two things are not the same and the whole first mesocycle exists to close the gap.

---

## 1. The governing principle: load management is the whole game

### Tissue tolerance vs applied load

Every running injury is the same equation. A tissue has a tolerance — a load it can absorb and
recover from — and running applies a load. Injury happens when applied load exceeds tolerance,
either in one dose or, far more often, as repeated doses that never get the recovery window
needed to adapt (Bertelsen et al. 2017). Both sides of that equation move. Tolerance rises with
progressive loading and falls with detraining, underfuelling, poor sleep and illness. Applied
load rises with distance, pace, gradient, surface and body mass.

That framing is not a metaphor. It's the actual decision rule:

| Situation | What moved | The intervention |
|---|---|---|
| Pain appearing 3-6 weeks into a build | Applied load outran tolerance | Cut load, hold the shape of the week |
| Pain after a layoff and quick return | Tolerance fell during the layoff | Rebuild from below the pre-layoff load |
| Pain after a new surface or shoe | Applied load redistributed | Revert, reintroduce one variable at a time |
| Pain with no load change | Tolerance fell — fuel, sleep, illness, stress | Look off the training log first |

**Most running injuries are training-load errors, not biomechanical faults.** Training error is
the most consistently implicated factor across the running-injury literature (Nielsen et al.
2012), and rapid progression specifically — increases beyond roughly 30% of the preceding
fortnight's distance — carries measurably higher risk of distance-related injury (Nielsen et al.
2014). Start every injury conversation with the training log, not the gait.

### Why gait, shoes and stretching are weaker levers

They are not zero. They are much smaller than load, and treating them as primary wastes the
weeks when load could have been fixed.

- **Running form.** Systematic review of prospective biomechanical risk factors finds limited
  and inconsistent evidence that any single kinematic variable predicts injury (Ceyssens et al.
  2019). Cross-sectional studies show injured runners move differently from uninjured ones, but
  that is as easily a consequence as a cause. Cadence is the one modifiable variable with a
  clear mechanical rationale (see §7) and even that is a symptom-modification tool, not a cure.
- **Pronation and footwear.** The pronation-control paradigm — matching shoe category to foot
  type to prevent injury — has not held up prospectively (Nigg et al. 2015). Comfort and
  familiarity predict tolerance better than any category label. Do not tell this athlete their
  arches caused the injury.
- **Static stretching.** No meaningful injury-prevention effect in running populations. Mobility
  work has a real job in this athlete's plan (§4 and the `strength-conditioning-coach` mobility
  block) — it just isn't injury prevention by stretching per se.
- **Strength training** is the exception among the non-load levers: it has genuine supporting
  evidence for reducing overuse injury and it's already in the plan twice a week. Protect it.

So when the athlete asks "is my form causing this", the honest answer is that training load is a
far more likely culprit and much cheaper to test. Change the load first. If symptoms persist
through a properly executed load reduction, then look wider.

## 2. Proximal hamstring in running — the flag that governs this athlete

### The conflict, stated openly

The strength block spent 22 weeks avoiding lengthened loading of the proximal hamstring. Running
does exactly that, involuntarily, several hundred times per kilometre — and at speed it does it
harder than any lift in the gym. Terminal swing puts the biceps femoris at or near peak
musculotendon length while it decelerates the shank eccentrically, and peak strain rises sharply
with running velocity (Schache et al. 2012). Separately, deep hip flexion compresses the
proximal tendon against the ischial tuberosity — the same mechanism that made seated leg curls a
stage-3 reward rather than a starting point (Goom, Malliaras & Reiman 2016).

So the return to running is the moment the block was protecting against, and it cannot be
avoided — running is the goal.

The resolution is the same one the lifting block used: **sequencing, not avoidance.** Easy
running at short stride length is a mild proximal hamstring stimulus and can start immediately
once the shared return-to-running gate in `strength-conditioning-coach/references/injury-
guardrails.md` §1 is met. Speed, stride length and downhills are added deliberately, one at a
time, late, and in a specific order. Nothing about the aerobic build requires fast running in
the first two mesocycles.

### Risk stimuli, ranked

Rank order for proximal hamstring provocation, highest first. Prescribe down this list, never up
it, and never introduce two adjacent tiers in the same week.

| Rank | Stimulus | Why it sits here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Downhill running, especially fast | Peak eccentric demand plus long stride plus high hip flexion |
| 2 | Flat sprinting / strides at near-max | Peak terminal-swing strain, rises steeply with velocity |
| 3 | Deliberately long strides, overstriding | Increases hip flexion at footstrike and swing-phase length |
| 4 | Fast tempo / threshold on the flat | Sustained moderate strain, high total exposure per session |
| 5 | Uphill running, including uphill sprints | High force, short stride, minimal lengthened-position strain |
| 6 | Easy running on the flat | Lowest strain per stride; the base of everything |

**Uphill sprints are the safe way to reintroduce high force.** Gradient shortens the stride and
truncates terminal swing, so the hamstring produces high force at a short-to-mid length rather
than peak force at peak length. That decoupling is the whole point: it lets neuromuscular
qualities and tendon load tolerance progress for 3-4 weeks before flat speed is ever attempted.
Start at 6-8% gradient, 8-10 seconds, full walk-down recovery, 4-6 reps.

### Introduction order for intensity

| Phase | Weeks from return | What's added | Gate to progress |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1-4 | Easy running only, conversational, flat-to-rolling | No symptoms at all for 2 weeks |
| B | 5-7 | Uphill strides, 6-8%, 8-10s, 4-6 reps, weekly | ≤3/10 during, clear next morning, 3 sessions |
| C | 8-10 | Flat strides on grass, 15-20s, controlled, sub-max | Clear for 2 weeks, no sitting pain |
| D | 11-14 | Tempo / threshold, flat, capped at 10% of weekly volume | Clear for 2 weeks at tempo |
| E | 14+ | Faster reps, longer strides, controlled downhill | Clear for 3 weeks; sequence downhill last |

Controlled downhill running is genuinely last. Not because downhill is forbidden forever, but
because it is the single highest-strain stimulus available and there is no reason to spend
tendon tolerance on it before the aerobic base is built.

### Pain-monitoring rule

Identical to the lifting rule, deliberately — the two skills must not give different numbers.

- **≤3/10 during the run, settled by the next morning → acceptable.** Continue as programmed.
- **>3/10 during the run, OR pain lingering into the next day, OR morning stiffness at the
  ischial tuberosity → regress.** Drop back one phase in the table above and hold for two weeks.
- **Pain on sitting, or sharp deep-buttock pain radiating down the back of the thigh → stop
  running.** Revert to isometrics per the strength skill's stage 1 and see §10.

Ask explicitly after speed and hill sessions. Do not wait to be told — proximal tendinopathy is
notorious for being tolerated silently until it isn't.

### The recurrence picture, honestly

Hamstring injuries have among the highest recurrence rates in running and sprint sport;
recurrence within a year is commonly reported around a third of cases, and the highest-risk
window is the first few weeks back. Most of that data comes from acute hamstring strain in team
sport rather than proximal tendinopathy in distance runners, so treat it as a direction of
travel rather than a precise number for this athlete. The consistent findings that do transfer:
returning with a residual strength deficit and returning straight to full-intensity exposure
both raise reinjury risk. Both are avoidable here.

### Return to running after a recurrence

If the hamstring flares badly enough to stop running, **the ladder back is the symptom-based
re-entry ladder in `return-to-run.md` §9.** It is kept in one place deliberately, so the two
files cannot give the athlete two different rungs for the same week.

Three clauses specific to a hamstring flare sit on top of it:

- **Rung 0 comes first: no running at all**, isometrics with the hip near neutral, typically 3-7
  days. Isometric loading is analgesic and tolerance-building, and it's the entry point for the
  same reason it is in the lifting skill's stage 1.
- **The ladder doesn't start until `strength-conditioning-coach` reports the hamstring back at
  stage 3-4** with the H-gate criteria met again. The running skill does not re-clear the
  hamstring on its own.
- **Re-enter at phase B (uphill strides), never at the phase the flare happened in.** Re-entering
  where it broke is the single most common way one flare becomes a season. Drop back at least a
  full phase, and rebuild volume to ~70% of pre-flare weekly volume before any intensity returns.

## 3. Bone stress injuries — highest consequence, most often missed

### Why they show up 3-8 weeks into a build

Bone adapts by remodelling, and remodelling is a two-stage process in which osteoclastic
resorption precedes osteoblastic formation. For several weeks after a meaningful load increase,
the bone in the loaded region is transiently *weaker* than it was before, because the resorption
cavities are open and the new bone hasn't been laid down and mineralised yet. That lag is why
bone stress injuries characteristically appear 3-8 weeks into a build rather than on the day of
the big week (Warden, Davis & Fredericson 2014).

Practical consequences that follow directly:

- The athlete who feels great in week 2 of a ramp is not evidence the ramp was safe. Judge the
  ramp at week 6.
- A build that steps up load and *holds* it for 3-4 weeks is safer than one that steps up every
  week, because the holding weeks let formation catch up.
- After a rest week or illness, the protective adaptation from the previous block is partly gone
  while the remodelling debt is not. This is the mechanism behind the make-up-week danger in §8.

### Risk factors

| Factor | Relevance here | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Rapid load increase | The primary driver, and the one we control | §8 rules are non-negotiable |
| Low energy availability | Directly suppresses bone formation | See §9; hand off to `sports-nutritionist` |
| Low vitamin D / calcium | Common in winter-hemisphere January builds | Nutrition skill owns intake; flag it |
| Prior bone stress injury | Strongest single predictor of another | Ask at intake, log the site |
| Menstrual dysfunction | Where relevant, a marker of low EA and low oestrogen | Refer; do not manage in-skill |
| Low bone mineral density | Often silent until the first BSI | Only relevant if previously measured |
| Very high weekly volume with low variability | Repetitive identical loading | Vary surface and route |

The prior-BSI point deserves emphasis: a runner who has had one is materially more likely to
have another, often at a different site, and that should tighten every progression rule below.

### Presentation — what distinguishes bone from soft tissue

| Feature | Bone stress | Tendon / muscle |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Focal, pinpoint, reproducible with one finger | Diffuse, hard to localise precisely |
| Behaviour through a run | Worsens as the run goes on | Often warms up and eases |
| Hop test | Pain on single-leg hopping | Usually tolerated |
| Night | Ache at rest or at night | Rare |
| Onset | Insidious, over 1-3 weeks | Often traceable to a session |

**Pain that gets worse through a run rather than better is the single most useful discriminator
and should trigger the bone question every time.** Runners are conditioned to expect things to
warm up; when something doesn't, take it seriously.

### High-risk vs lower-risk sites

This distinction changes management completely and must not be blurred.

| Risk | Sites | Why | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | Femoral neck, anterior tibial cortex, navicular, sacrum, base of 2nd metatarsal, talus, medial malleolus | Tension-side or poor blood supply; risk of non-union, delayed healing, or complete fracture | Stop running. Urgent imaging (MRI). Medical management before any return |
| Lower | Posteromedial tibia, metatarsals 2-4 (shaft), fibula, calcaneus, femoral shaft | Compression-side, good healing environment | Still stop running. Relative rest, graded return, imaging if not settling |

Anterior tibial cortex pain in particular is easy to mistake for shin splints and carries a very
different consequence — see §6 for the discrimination.

### The absolute rule

**Suspected bone stress means stop running and get imaged. It never means "run through it
easy".** There is no easy-pace version of a femoral neck stress fracture that ends well. If the
presentation fits — focal bone tenderness, worsening through the run, positive hop test — the
prescription is cross-training and a referral, and the skill says that plainly even if it costs
the race. X-ray is frequently negative early; a negative X-ray does not clear a suspected BSI,
and MRI is the appropriate investigation.

## 4. Achilles and calf — the most under-prepared structure on return

Twenty-two weeks without running means the plantarflexor complex has not absorbed impact at
running frequencies for five months. Calf raises in the gym build the muscle; they do not build
the tendon's tolerance for thousands of stretch-shortening cycles at 2.5-3× bodyweight. This is
the structure most likely to complain in weeks 3-8, and it compounds with the `ankleMobility`
flag.

### Insertional vs mid-portion — the distinction that changes management

| | Mid-portion | Insertional |
|---|---|---|
| Site | 2-6 cm above the calcaneus | At the calcaneal attachment |
| Palpation | Tender through the tendon body, often thickened | Tender at the bone-tendon junction |
| Aggravated by | Volume, speed, hills | Dorsiflexion under load — the tendon is compressed against the calcaneus |
| Loading range | Full range, including below neutral, is fine | **Do not load into dorsiflexion below neutral early** |
| Practical set-up | Heel raises off a step, full stretch at the bottom | Heel raises on flat floor only; heel lift in shoes helps |
| Hills | Uphill is the aggravator (high force) | Uphill is worse still (force plus dorsiflexion) |

The insertional rule is the one that gets missed. Prescribing off-a-step eccentric heel drops
for insertional Achilles pain reliably makes it worse, because the compressive component is the
problem, not the tensile one. **For insertional pain, cap the bottom of the range at neutral
until symptoms settle, then extend it over weeks.**

### Heavy slow resistance loading

Heavy slow resistance is at least as effective as eccentric-only protocols for Achilles
tendinopathy, with better adherence and patient satisfaction (Beyer et al. 2015). It also
integrates cleanly with the two weekly lifting sessions rather than requiring a separate daily
rehab habit.

| Element | Prescription | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 3 sessions/week, at least a day apart | Tendon protein synthesis stays elevated ~36-72h |
| Movements | Standing heel raise, seated heel raise, leg press calf press | Seated biases soleus, which carries most running load |
| Tempo | ~3s up, ~3s down, 6s per rep | Time under tension is the active ingredient |
| Load | Week 1: 3 × 15RM, progressing to 4 × 6RM by week 9-12 | Progressive overload, not a fixed weight |
| Duration | 12 weeks minimum | Tendon adaptation is slow; 4 weeks is not a trial |
| Range | Full, including below neutral — *unless insertional* | See table above |

Note the overlap with the strength skill's calf work: full-range calf raises with a bottom pause
serve calf hypertrophy, ankle dorsiflexion, and Achilles tolerance simultaneously. Do not
duplicate the volume across skills — coordinate.

### Morning-stiffness monitoring rule

The tendon-specific monitoring variable is **morning stiffness on the first ten steps out of
bed**, tracked as a trend rather than a single reading:

- Stiffness stable or reducing week over week → the current load is being tolerated.
- Stiffness increasing over 7-10 days → the load is too high even if the runs feel fine.
- Stiffness that appears where there was none → treat as a yellow light, hold volume flat.

Silbernagel's pain-monitoring model permits running to continue with Achilles pain up to a
tolerable level provided it settles by the next morning and morning stiffness does not trend
upward over weeks (Silbernagel et al. 2007). That model is more permissive than the ≤3/10 rule
we use for the proximal hamstring, deliberately — Achilles tendinopathy responds well to
continued loading, whereas the hamstring flag here is compressive in nature and less forgiving.
Keep the two rules distinct and don't let the athlete apply the looser one to the hamstring.

### Ankle dorsiflexion restriction as a contributing factor

This athlete carries the `ankleMobility` flag. Restricted dorsiflexion means the shank cannot
travel forward over the foot during midstance, so the movement is redistributed — commonly into
early heel lift, increased calf demand, or compensatory pronation. It also constrains the
ability to absorb downhill and stair loading.

- Keep the knee-to-wall measurement running through the run build, both sides, every 2 weeks.
  It is the same progress marker the strength skill uses; share the number.
- **Do not add uphill volume in a week where dorsiflexion has regressed.** Uphill running demands
  dorsiflexion range the ankle may not have, and takes it from the Achilles.
- Loaded end-range work retains range better than passive stretching. The calf raise with a
  bottom pause is doing double duty already.

### Progression to plyometrics

Plyometrics belong after the tendon has demonstrated tolerance to heavy slow loading, not
before. Rough gate: pain-free single-leg heel raises to fatigue at a sensible rep count with
symmetry between sides, and 4 weeks of stable running volume. Then pogo hops → double-leg
skipping → single-leg hops → bounding, one step every 1-2 weeks, low volume, on a forgiving
surface. This work is the direct bridge to strides and downhill running in §2, so sequence it
ahead of them.

## 5. Patellofemoral pain and the knee flag

### Presentation

Diffuse anterior knee pain, poorly localised — the athlete usually cups the kneecap rather than
pointing to a spot. Aggravated by descending stairs and hills, prolonged sitting, squatting, and
the accumulation of running volume rather than any single session. Often bilateral or alternating
sides. Crepitus is common and, on its own, meaningless.

Discriminators worth checking before settling on the label: true joint-line pain, locking,
giving way, or a swollen joint are not patellofemoral pain and go to §10.

### Load management first

Same logic as everywhere else. The reliable pattern is that symptoms track cumulative volume and
descent, so those are the two dials:

| Modifier | Effect | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce total weekly volume 20-30% | Directly reduces cumulative patellofemoral load | Symptoms rising over 2+ weeks |
| Remove or flatten downhill | Descent is the highest patellofemoral load per step | Downhill is the identifiable trigger |
| Increase cadence 5-10% | Shortens stride, reduces per-step knee load (Heiderscheit et al. 2011) | Short-term symptom modification |
| Shorten individual runs, keep frequency | Same weekly volume, lower peak per session | Symptoms appear late in longer runs |
| Substitute cycling / pool running | Maintains aerobic load, much lower patellofemoral load | Need to hold fitness during a down-phase |

Cadence is a symptom-modifier, not a fix: it shortens stride but adds step count, so load moves
rather than disappears. Use it for 3-6 weeks while the load problem is corrected, then let it go.

### The strength side

Exercise therapy is the best-supported intervention for patellofemoral pain, and combined hip
and knee targeted exercise outperforms knee-focused exercise alone (Collins et al. 2018; Barton
et al. 2015). Both components already exist in the athlete's lifting sessions:

- **Hip abductors and external rotators** — the hip-targeted half. Abduction machine work,
  banded lateral walks, side-lying abduction, single-leg hip thrust.
- **Quadriceps strength through a pain-free range** — leg press and hack squat to tolerance,
  heel-elevated squat patterns, Spanish squats and leg-extension isometrics on flare days.

Isometric quad loading is analgesic for anterior knee pain and is the first thing to reach for
when symptoms spike; it lets the athlete keep loading the quad rather than detraining it.

### Interaction with the existing knee flag

The `knee` flag came out of the lifting block, where it was managed by capping deep-knee-flexion
volume and building quads through leg press and hack squat in a pain-free range. Running changes
the exposure profile: lower peak flexion angles, vastly higher repetition count, and a new
eccentric demand on descent that the gym never provided.

Rules for the overlap:

- **Do not let the knee take a new stressor from both skills in the same week.** If the lifting
  block is expanding squat depth, the run week does not add hills, and vice versa. Coordinate
  explicitly with `strength-conditioning-coach` rather than assuming.
- Downhill running is a *knee* stressor as well as a hamstring stressor. It sits last in the
  progression for two independent reasons.
- If knee symptoms flare, cut running volume and drop to isometric quad loading in the gym —
  do not stop lower-body lifting entirely. Detraining the quad makes patellofemoral pain worse.
- Stair and descent tolerance is a useful weekly check-in question and costs nothing to ask.

## 6. Other common presentations

| Condition | Presentation | Likely load driver | First-line management | Escalate if |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ITB syndrome** | Sharp lateral knee pain ~2-3 cm above the joint line, comes on at a predictable time into a run, worse downhill and on cambered roads | Sudden volume increase, downhill running, always running the same side of a cambered road, high weekly volume with low variability | Reduce volume and remove downhill; vary the route and running direction; hip abductor and external rotator strengthening; short-term cadence increase | Pain at rest, pain on stairs, or no improvement after 3-4 weeks of load reduction |
| **Plantar heel pain** | Sharp pain under the medial heel, worst on the first steps in the morning and after sitting, eases then returns late in a run | Rapid volume increase, new footwear with less heel drop, calf tightness, sudden addition of speed work | High-load plantar-fascia-specific loading — heel raise with the toes extended over a towel, slow tempo, 3×/week (Rathleff et al. 2015); calf loading; short-term shoe with more heel drop | Numbness or burning (suggests nerve), heel pain at night, or focal pinpoint calcaneal tenderness (calcaneal BSI) |
| **Tibialis posterior tendinopathy** | Pain and sometimes swelling behind and below the medial malleolus, worse on toe-off, single-leg heel raise painful or weak | Volume increase, hills, restricted dorsiflexion forcing compensatory pronation | Reduce volume and hills; heavy slow resistance for tib post and calf; address the dorsiflexion restriction; temporary arch support | Inability to perform a single-leg heel raise, or a progressive flattening of the arch — needs assessment |
| **MTSS ("shin splints")** | Diffuse ache along the posteromedial border of the tibia, spread over ≥5 cm, tender to broad palpation, often eases as the run warms up | Rapid mileage increase after a layoff — the classic return-to-running injury; hard surfaces; new shoes | Reduce volume 30-50% and hold; keep frequency; soft surfaces; calf and tib post strengthening; rebuild slowly | Pain becomes focal and pinpoint, worsens through the run, hurts at night, or hop test is positive — that's bone stress, see below |
| **Hip flexor / adductor** | Anterior hip or groin pain, worse on the drive phase and on hills or speed; adductor pain worse on change of direction and on the push-off | Speed and hill work introduced too early; tight hip flexors limiting extension so the flexor works against its own restriction | Reduce speed and hills; Copenhagen adductor progression; hip flexor loading through range; continue the mobility block | Groin pain with a sense of instability, night pain, or pain referred from the hip joint itself — pubic or femoral neck BSI must be excluded |
| **Gluteal tendinopathy** | Lateral hip pain over the greater trochanter, worse lying on that side at night, worse on single-leg stance and stairs | Volume increase, running on cambered surfaces, adducted running posture, prolonged sitting cross-legged | Education plus progressive abductor loading is superior to injection or wait-and-see (Mellor et al. 2018); avoid compressive positions — crossing legs, hanging on one hip in standing, side-lying on the painful side | No improvement over 6-8 weeks, or radiating pain suggesting lumbar referral |

### The MTSS / bone stress relationship, stated explicitly

MTSS and tibial bone stress injury sit on the same continuum of bone-related overload, and MTSS
that is ignored can progress. They are distinguished by how the pain behaves, not by how bad it
feels:

| | MTSS | Tibial BSI |
|---|---|---|
| Tenderness | Diffuse, ≥5 cm along the posteromedial border | Focal, pinpoint, one finger |
| Through a run | Often warms up and eases | Worsens as the run progresses |
| Hop test | Usually negative or mildly sore | Positive |
| Night pain | No | Sometimes |
| Site | Posteromedial (compression side) | Posteromedial, or **anterior cortex** — the tension side |

**Anterior tibial cortex pain is a high-risk site and is not shin splints.** It sits on the
tension side of the bone, heals badly, and is the classic "dreaded black line" on imaging. Any
focal anterior shin pain stops running and gets imaged. Do not negotiate this one.

## 7. Managing a niggle without losing the build

Most niggles do not need the build abandoned. They need one variable changed for one to three
weeks. The failure mode in both directions is common: stopping entirely and losing four weeks of
fitness, or ignoring it until it becomes an injury that costs twelve.

### The traffic-light system

| Light | Signal | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Green** | Pain ≤2/10, not worsening within the run, gone by next morning, gait normal | Proceed as programmed. Log it and monitor. Ask again next session |
| **Amber** | Pain 3-4/10, or worsening within a run, or present the next morning | Modify one variable — volume, surface, gradient or intensity. Hold the modified week for at least 7 days |
| **Red** | Pain >4/10, or altering gait, or worsening session on session | Stop that session. Cross-train. Reassess in 48h. Two consecutive red sessions → §10 |

**Altering gait is a red light regardless of the number on the pain scale.** A limp redistributes
load to structures that have not been prepared for it, which is how one injury becomes two.

### Cross-training substitutions to hold fitness

Fitness is not lost quickly; it is lost by panicking. Substitute by aerobic equivalence.

| Wanted | Substitute | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Easy run | Cycling, 1.5× the duration | No impact, similar aerobic stimulus, minimal tendon load |
| Long run | Long ride, or pool running | Preserves the aerobic long-duration stimulus without cumulative impact |
| Tempo run | Pool running at effort, or bike threshold | Metabolically specific; removes impact and eccentric load entirely |
| Strides | Uphill strides (hamstring-permitting), or bike sprints | High force, minimal lengthened-position strain |
| Hill session | Bike hill repeats, or incline walking | Retains the force and cardiac stimulus, drops impact |
| Any session (bone stress suspected) | Pool running or cycling only — **not** elliptical or incline walking | Any weight-bearing impact is contraindicated until cleared |

Pool running is the closest substitute for running-specific fitness and the most tedious. Say so
honestly rather than pretending it's fun.

### Short-term modifiers

| Modifier | Reduces | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Softer surface (trail, grass, track) | Peak impact load; useful for MTSS and bone-adjacent pain | Uneven ground adds ankle demand — bad with the `ankleMobility` flag on technical trail |
| Remove downhill | Eccentric quad and hamstring load; patellofemoral load | Loses descent-specific tolerance; must be rebuilt later |
| Add uphill | Nothing — this *increases* calf, Achilles and hip flexor load | Only a substitution for hamstring-driven problems |
| Cadence +5-10% | Per-step knee and hip load | Increases step count; calf and Achilles load may rise |
| Shorter, more frequent runs | Peak per-session load at the same weekly volume | Costs long-run specific adaptation |
| Reduce intensity, hold volume | Peak tissue strain | Slower fitness progression, but the safest first move |

### One variable at a time

**Change one thing per week.** If the athlete simultaneously drops volume, changes shoes, moves
to trail and adds cadence work, and the pain resolves, nothing has been learned — and when it
comes back, there is no information to act on. Change one variable, hold it for 7 days, record
the result. This is slower for one week and dramatically faster over a season.

## 8. Load-management rules that prevent most of this

### The core rules

| Rule | Specification | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **3 up / 1 down** | Three progressive weeks, then a week at ~60-70% volume | Gives the bone remodelling cycle (§3) a window for formation to catch up |
| **One new stressor at a time** | Volume, intensity, hills, surface, shoes — one per week, never two | Otherwise a flare cannot be attributed and the next build repeats the error |
| **Time on feet before pace** | Progress duration to target, then progress intensity within it | Duration builds tendon and bone tolerance at low strain; pace raises strain steeply |
| **Weekly increase cap** | ~10% as a default; hard ceiling near 30% over any fortnight | >30% fortnightly progression is associated with higher injury risk (Nielsen et al. 2014) |
| **Frequency before duration** | 5 × 30 min beats 3 × 50 min in the first mesocycle | More frequent, smaller doses build tolerance with lower per-session peaks |
| **Intensity capped as a share of volume** | Hard running ≤10% of weekly volume in the first 12 weeks | Keeps the base aerobic and limits high-strain exposure |
| **No new stressor in a deload week** | Down weeks are down weeks | The point is recovery, not a chance to sneak in hills |

### The 7-day : 28-day load ratio, with honest caveats

A common guideline is to keep the ratio of this week's load to the rolling 4-week average
between about 0.8 and 1.3-1.5. Popularised by the acute:chronic workload ratio literature
(Gabbett 2016), it is a reasonable sanity check and a useful conversational tool.

It is also methodologically contested. Subsequent critiques identified serious problems with the
ACWR — mathematical coupling between numerator and denominator, arbitrary binning of ratio
values, and the "sweet spot" being partly an artefact of the analysis rather than a biological
finding (Impellizzeri et al. 2020). Treat it accordingly:

- **Use it as a guardrail, not a prescription.** A ratio of 1.6 is a prompt to look at the week,
  not proof of impending injury. A ratio of 1.0 is not permission to ignore symptoms.
- Prefer it as a way to catch obvious spikes — the 1.8-week after a missed week — which is where
  it is least controversial and most useful.
- Never override a symptom report with a favourable ratio. The athlete's morning stiffness is
  better data than the model.

### The make-up week

The most reliably dangerous week in any build is the one after a missed week. The athlete feels
behind, the plan says 60 km, they did 20 km last week, and the instinct is to run 70 to catch up.
This produces a load spike at exactly the moment tissue tolerance has dropped, and it is the
single most common origin story for both bone stress injuries and Achilles flares.

**There is no such thing as making up missed training.** The rule:

| Time missed | Return at | Then |
|---|---|---|
| 1-3 days | Where the plan left off | Normal progression |
| 4-7 days | ~80% of the last completed week | Rebuild over 1-2 weeks |
| 1-2 weeks | ~70% of the last completed week, no intensity in week 1 | Rebuild over 2-3 weeks |
| 2-4 weeks | ~50-60%, easy only, frequency before duration | Rebuild over 3-4 weeks |
| >4 weeks | Treat as a fresh return to running; restart the §2 phase table | Full re-progression |

Say the number out loud when the athlete asks to make it up. The plan's goal is arriving at the
race, not completing the spreadsheet.

## 9. Energy availability and running injury

### Why underfuelling multiplies injury risk

Low energy availability — insufficient energy left over for physiological function after
exercise energy expenditure — impairs bone formation, suppresses reproductive and thyroid
hormones, and degrades recovery and adaptation across the board. The IOC's RED-S consensus work
places bone stress injury among the best-documented consequences (Mountjoy et al. 2018/2023).
Tendon and muscle adaptation depend on the same energy and protein availability.

The mechanism matters for how it presents. Underfuelling does not cause a distinct injury type;
it lowers tissue tolerance across the board, so the athlete gets injured at loads they previously
handled and the training log looks innocent. When symptoms appear with no load change, fuelling
is the first place to look.

### The specific risk in this build

This is the interaction that matters most for this athlete, and it is worth stating explicitly:

**A bulk ending while mileage climbs is the dangerous combination.** The athlete has spent 22
weeks in a calorie surplus. January brings two simultaneous changes — the surplus comes off, and
running energy expenditure climbs from near zero. If the intake reduction is applied at the same
time as the mileage increase, energy availability can fall hard and fast without anyone
intentionally dieting. The athlete will read the resulting weight loss as the bulk unwinding
successfully rather than as a fuelling deficit.

The resolution, again, is sequencing:

- Do not reduce calories in the same week that running volume increases. Stagger them.
- Expect maintenance intake to *rise*, not fall, as mileage climbs, even if the goal is to lose
  the bulk's fat gain. The surplus comes off by not adding to it, not by cutting hard.
- Watch for the fuelling warning signs: rapid weight loss, persistent fatigue disproportionate
  to training, poor sleep, elevated resting heart rate, recurrent illness, low mood, and
  stalled or reversing performance at stable training load.
- Fuel the long run and the hard sessions specifically. Running long fasted while building
  mileage and coming off a surplus is the worst combination of the three.

### Handoff

`sports-nutritionist` owns intake targets, the surplus taper and the rate-of-change guardrails.
This skill owns the observation that something is wrong and the size of the training load being
fuelled. When flagging a fuelling concern, hand over three specific things: current and
projected weekly running volume, the last four weeks of body-mass trend, and the specific
symptoms observed. Do not set macro targets from this skill, and do not let the nutrition skill
set running volume.

## 10. Red flags — stop and refer

Programming stops and the athlete sees a clinician if any of these appear:

- **Suspected bone stress at a high-risk site** — focal anterior shin pain, groin or deep hip
  pain worse on weight-bearing, midfoot pain over the navicular, or sacral or low-buttock pain
  with a positive hop test. Stop running, refer, image. Not negotiable.
- Sharp deep-buttock pain radiating down the posterior thigh, worse on sitting — proximal
  hamstring tendinopathy that has progressed beyond load management.
- Any neurological symptoms — numbness, pins and needles, weakness, or foot drop. That is neural
  and load management will not fix it.
- Calf pain with swelling, warmth, redness, or unilateral oedema — possible DVT. This is urgent
  and is not a calf strain until a clinician says so.
- Chest pain, syncope or near-syncope, palpitations, or exertional breathlessness
  disproportionate to the effort. Stop the session and seek same-day medical assessment.
- Night pain, or pain unrelated to loading.
- Any sudden "pop" during a run with immediate weakness or bruising.
- True joint swelling, locking, or the knee giving way.
- Pain that has not settled after two weeks of appropriate load reduction.

Say this plainly when it applies. Do not program through it, and do not soften it because a race
is close.

## 11. Evidence base

- **Bertelsen et al. (2017)**, *A framework for the etiology of running-related injuries*, Scand
  J Med Sci Sports — the tissue-tolerance-vs-cumulative-load model used throughout §1.
- **Nielsen et al. (2012)**, IJSPT, and **Nielsen et al. (2014)**, JOSPT — training error as the
  most consistently implicated factor in running injury, and progression beyond ~30% of the
  preceding fortnight's distance as a specific risk. The basis for the §8 ceiling.
- **Ceyssens et al. (2019)**, *Biomechanical risk factors associated with running-related
  injuries: a systematic review*, Sports Medicine — limited and inconsistent prospective evidence
  that kinematic variables predict injury. The reason form is a weak lever in §1.
- **Nigg, Baltich, Hoerzer & Enders (2015)**, *Running shoes and running injuries: mythbusting
  and a proposal for two new paradigms*, BJSM — the pronation-control paradigm is not supported;
  comfort and preferred movement path matter more than shoe category.
- **Goom, Malliaras & Reiman (2016)**, *Proximal hamstring tendinopathy: clinical aspects of
  assessment and management*, JOSPT — staged loading and the tendon-compression-at-hip-flexion
  mechanism. Shared with `strength-conditioning-coach`.
- **Schache et al. (2012)**, *Mechanics of the human hamstring muscles during sprinting*, MSSE —
  peak biceps femoris musculotendon strain occurs in terminal swing and rises with velocity. The
  reason speed sits near the top of the §2 risk ranking.
- **Warden, Davis & Fredericson (2014)**, *Management and prevention of bone stress injuries in
  long-distance runners*, JOSPT — remodelling lag, high- vs low-risk sites, graded return.
- **Beyer et al. (2015)**, AJSM, and **Silbernagel et al. (2007)**, AJSM — heavy slow resistance
  matches eccentric-only training for Achilles tendinopathy with better adherence, and running
  may continue under a pain-monitoring model with morning stiffness as the governing variable.
  Together these are the §4 protocol.
- **Collins et al. (2018)**, *Patellofemoral pain consensus statement from the 4th International
  Patellofemoral Pain Research Retreat*, BJSM, and **Barton et al. (2015)**, *Best practice guide
  to conservative management of patellofemoral pain*, BJSM — combined hip-and-knee exercise
  therapy outperforms knee-focused exercise alone.
- **Heiderscheit et al. (2011)**, *Effects of step rate manipulation on joint mechanics during
  running*, MSSE — a 5-10% cadence increase reduces load at the hip and knee. Used as a
  short-term symptom modifier, not a cure.
- **Rathleff et al. (2015)**, Scand J Med Sci Sports, and **Mellor et al. (2018)**, BMJ (LEAP
  trial) — high-load strength training beat stretching for plantar heel pain at 3 months, and
  education plus progressive exercise beat injection and wait-and-see for gluteal tendinopathy.
  Both underwrite the "load it, don't rest it" first-line column in §6.
- **Gabbett (2016)**, *The training-injury prevention paradox*, BJSM, read alongside
  **Impellizzeri et al. (2020)**, *Acute:chronic workload ratio: conceptual issues and
  fundamental pitfalls*, IJSPP — the ratio guideline and the methodological critique that keeps
  it a guardrail rather than a prescription.
- **Mountjoy et al. (2018; updated 2023)**, *IOC consensus statement on Relative Energy
  Deficiency in Sport*, BJSM — low energy availability, impaired bone health, and elevated bone
  stress injury risk. The evidence behind §9 and the handoff to `sports-nutritionist`.
