# Situations

The model in `lean-bulk-protocol.md` assumes a normal week. Most weeks aren't. This file covers
the ones that break it — a restriction that rules out half the food list, four nights in a
hotel, a wedding, a stomach bug, a fortnight where nothing tastes of anything, and a weight
trend that says something the body isn't doing. The through-line is the same everywhere: **keep
protein, protect sleep, keep logging.** A disrupted week costs almost nothing across a 5-month
block. Abandoning the log for a fortnight costs the ability to steer.

## 1. Dietary restrictions and preferences

A restriction is a hard constraint on every suggestion afterwards, not a preference to route
around. Work out what it actually costs first. In almost every case it's the same two things:
**protein density** — grams of protein per calorie and per mouthful — and **two or three
specific micronutrients**. Everything else is substitution.

| Restriction | The real cost | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Vegetarian** | Protein density. Without planning the diet drifts to carbs | Anchor 4 feeds on dairy, egg and soya; isolate powder for the gap |
| **Vegan** | Protein density, leucine per gram, digestibility. Plus B12, iron, zinc, iodine, omega-3, creatine | Higher per-meal doses, isolate powder, a short fixed supplement list |
| **Dairy-free** | Loses the best convenience protein (yoghurt, milk, whey, cottage cheese) and ~700 mg/day calcium | Fortified soya products, pea/beef/egg isolate, tinned fish with bones |
| **Gluten-free** | Not protein — **carbs**. 440 g/day is hard without bread, pasta and cereal | Rice, potatoes, certified GF oats, corn, buckwheat, GF pasta, dextrose |
| **Nut allergy** | The cheapest fat lever disappears | Seeds and seed butters, olive oil, avocado, oily fish, full-fat dairy |
| **Shellfish allergy** | Minor. Prawns are convenient, not essential | Any other protein |
| **Egg allergy** | Breakfast protein. Check whey blends and protein bars for egg white | Greek yoghurt, cottage cheese, tofu scramble, leftover dinner meat |

### Plant protein reference

| Food | serving | P | kcal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Firm tofu | 200 g | 34 | 290 |
| Tempeh | 150 g | 28 | 285 |
| Seitan | 100 g | 25 | 150 |

| Red lentils (cooked) | 250 g | 22 | 290 |
| Chickpeas (cooked) | 250 g | 22 | 410 |
| Black beans (cooked) | 250 g | 22 | 330 |
| Soya milk (fortified) | 500 ml | 17 | 220 |
| Pea protein isolate | 30 g | 24 | 115 |

| Quinoa (cooked) | 250 g | 11 | 300 |
| Hemp seeds | 30 g | 9 | 165 |
| Pumpkin seeds | 30 g | 9 | 170 |
| Lentil / chickpea pasta (dry) | 100 g | 25 | 340 |

Read the shape of that table: 40 g of protein costs ~750 kcal from chickpeas, ~340 from tofu,
~190 from isolate powder. On a surplus the calories have somewhere to go, but the day fills up
with legumes and fibre long before protein is met. That's the mechanism behind most "I can't
hit protein on a vegan diet" weeks, and it's why an isolate powder is close to mandatory.

### The leucine threshold, and why plant doses run higher

MPS is triggered per feed, and the trigger is leucine-sensitive — a feed needs to clear roughly
2.5-3 g of leucine to switch on a full response. Two things work against plant protein. First,
the leucine fraction is lower: whey is ~10-11% leucine by protein weight, soya and pea ~7-8%,
wheat lower again (Gorissen et al. 2018), so 30 g of whey delivers ~2.7 g leucine and 30 g of
pea delivers ~1.9 g. Second, digestibility is lower and the amino acid profile is incomplete —
legumes are short on methionine, grains on lysine (van Vliet, Burd & van Loon 2015).

The correction is arithmetic, not philosophical:

- **Per feed: 0.5-0.6 g/kg rather than 0.4.** At 80 kg that's **40-48 g**, not 32 — roughly a
  1.2-1.5× multiplier on the animal-protein dose.
- **Per day: 2.2 g/kg rather than 2.0.** At 80 kg, **176 g**. Cheap insurance against
  digestibility losses compounding across four or five feeds.
- **Mix sources within the meal**, not just across the day — legume plus grain, or anything
  plus 25 g of isolate. Combining within the feed is what fixes leucine for *that* feed.
- **Soya is the exception**: closest plant protein to animal on both leucine and digestibility.
  If one source anchors the day, make it soya.

**Common failure mode:** 176 g hit on paper via four 44 g doses, three of them lentils and rice
at 700 kcal each, and the day is 800 kcal over by dinner. Whole-food legumes for two feeds,
isolate for the other two.

### Vegan micronutrients that genuinely need attention

Only these. The rest looks after itself on a varied plant diet with enough calories.

| Nutrient | Why | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **B12** | No reliable unfortified plant source; deficiency is neurological, slow and silent | Supplement. 10-25 µg daily or 2,000 µg weekly. Non-negotiable |
| **Iron** | Non-haem iron absorbs at roughly a third the rate of haem | Vitamin C with iron-containing meals; no tea or coffee alongside them — polyphenols inhibit absorption (Hurrell & Egli 2010) |
| **Zinc** | Phytates in legumes and wholegrains bind it | Soak or sprout legumes; pumpkin seeds and cashews. 10-15 mg supplement if intake is thin |
| **Iodine** | Plant milks carry a fraction of the iodine in cow's milk — a real UK gap (Bath et al. 2017) | Iodised salt or 150 µg. Seaweed works but the dose is wildly variable |
| **Omega-3** | ALA→EPA→DHA conversion runs at single-digit percentages | Algal oil, 250-500 mg combined EPA+DHA. Flax and chia don't replace it |
| **Calcium** | ~700 mg/day, and dairy was doing the work | Fortified soya/oat milk (organic lines often aren't fortified), calcium-set tofu, kale, tahini |
| **Creatine** | Dietary creatine comes from meat and fish, so baseline muscle stores run lower | 3-5 g/day monohydrate. Vegetarians often respond more than omnivores (Burke et al. 2003). Expect the ~1 kg water bump — see §9 |

### Dairy-free and gluten-free swaps

| Instead of | Use | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 30 g whey | 30 g pea, soya or beef isolate | Same protein, worse taste. Blend with banana |
| 250 g Greek yoghurt | High-protein soya yoghurt | Coconut yoghurt is ~2 g P per pot — a dessert, not a protein source |
| 500 ml milk | 500 ml fortified soya milk | 17 g P either way. Almond and oat milk are ~2 g P — carb drinks |
| Cottage cheese | Firm tofu, pressed and salted | Same role, similar macros |
| Cheese on everything | Nutritional yeast + olive oil | The calories come from the oil, not the yeast |
| Pasta / bread | Rice, potatoes, GF pasta, corn tortillas, oatcakes | Cheapest route back to 440 g carbs. GF loaves run 30-40% smaller per slice |
| Oats | Certified gluten-free oats | Oats are naturally GF; contamination is the issue |
| Couscous / bulgur | Quinoa, rice, polenta | Straight swap |

### "I just don't like X"

Treat it exactly as an allergy. A plan built on disliked food is executed for two weeks,
resented in week three and abandoned in week four — and what gets abandoned is usually the whole
structure, not one food. The useful question isn't "can you learn to eat it" but "what job was
it doing" — cheap protein per calorie, convenience, or bulk. Replace the job.

Worth naming alongside it: the same meal five days running kills more prep routines than
anything else. Cook one protein and one carb in bulk and vary the sauce. Three sauces makes the
same chicken and rice feel like three meals.

## 2. Travel

**Set the target correctly first: on a trip the goal is maintenance and an unbroken log, not
progress.** Hitting 3,500 kcal and 160 g of protein across an airport, a plane, a hotel and a
client dinner is possible, but it costs attention most trips don't have spare. Hold protein,
let calories land where they land, come home flat rather than down. A maintenance week inside a
5-month block is nothing; a week off the log that becomes a fortnight is the real risk.

### What to pack

Pack for protein — that's what airports and petrol stations are worst at. Carbs are everywhere
and fat is unavoidable.

| Item | Pack | kcal | P | C | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whey sachets | 6 × 30 g | 115 ea | 25 | 2 | 2 |
| Beef jerky / biltong | 4 × 50 g | 160 ea | 30 | 6 | 2 |
| Protein bars | 4-6 | 220 ea | 20 | 22 | 7 |
| Mixed nuts | 200 g | 620/100 g | 20 | 12 | 55 |
| Instant oat sachets | 4 × 60 g | 228 ea | 8 | 36 | 4 |
| Oatcakes | 1 pack (~24) | 45 ea | 1 | 6 | 2 |

That's roughly 450 g of protein and 5,600 kcal in about 1.4 kg of luggage. Add a 700 ml
shaker — highest-value item on the list, because without one the whey sachets don't get used.
**Common failure mode:** packing it all and never opening the bag because the trip's meals were
fine. That's the correct outcome. It's insurance, not a plan.

### Airport and petrol station buys

| Buy | kcal | P | C | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Supermarket rotisserie chicken, half | 500 | 65 | 0 | 26 |
| Chicken and rice/pasta pot (M&S, Boots) | 400 | 30 | 45 | 8 |
| Chicken caesar wrap | 450 | 30 | 40 | 18 |
| Sushi tray, salmon/tuna, 8-10 pieces | 450 | 22 | 65 | 10 |
| Ready-to-drink protein shake, 500 ml | 250 | 30 | 20 | 4 |
| 500 ml semi-skimmed milk | 250 | 17 | 24 | 9 |
| Tinned tuna + crackers | 350 | 32 | 30 | 10 |
| Boiled egg pot (2 eggs) | 140 | 12 | 1 | 10 |
| 0% Greek yoghurt pot, 170 g | 100 | 17 | 6 | 0 |
| Beef jerky, 50 g | 160 | 30 | 6 | 2 |
| Malt loaf / flapjack | 300 | 5 | 50 | 9 |
| Banana + honey sachet | 130 | 1 | 32 | 0 |

Read labels in this order: **grams of protein per pack**, then calories. Under 15 g of protein
is a carb-and-fat purchase — buy it if calories are short, not if protein is. Supermarket
rotisserie chicken is the best protein-per-pound buy near any airport or motorway services.

### Hotel room, kettle and fridge

A kettle handles instant oats (60 g sachet + 200 ml hot water, whey stirred in **after** it
cools slightly — whey in boiling water goes lumpy), instant rice pouches stood in hot water,
couscous, and instant noodles bulked with a jerky pack. A fridge handles milk, Greek yoghurt,
cottage cheese, supermarket cooked chicken and lentil pouches. With neither: tinned tuna, jerky,
nuts, UHT milk, ambient rice, oatcakes and peanut butter sachets.

Worked evening with no room service: 2 × 60 g oat sachets, 30 g whey, 500 ml milk, 40 g nuts
= ~1,150 kcal, 65 g protein.

### Hotel breakfast

**The buffet is the single best protein opportunity of the day — build the day around it.**
Lunch and dinner get decided by other people; breakfast is the one meal fully under control,
and it's unlimited. Target 60 g of protein: 4-5 eggs or a large omelette, 100-150 g of
whatever cooked meat is out, 200 g of natural yoghurt, plus oats or toast. That's ~65 g protein
and ~1,000 kcal before 9am, which means a picked-at lunch and a restaurant dinner still land the
day near target. Continental spread only: yoghurt, milk, cold meats, cheese, plus a whey scoop
from the bag. Pastries are calories, not protein — take them if calories are short.

### A 10-hour flight

- **Eat properly before boarding.** A real 800-1,000 kcal meal in the terminal beats anything
  served at 35,000 ft, and makes the airline meal a bonus rather than the plan.
- **Two whey sachets, jerky and nuts through security.** Crew will fill a shaker with water —
  ~90 g of protein independent of the catering.
- **~250 ml fluid per hour.** Cabin air is very dry, and the resulting deficit is the largest
  contributor to arriving wrecked. It also reads as a 1-2 kg "loss" for two days (§9).
- **Alcohol on a plane is the worst-value drink of the trip** — it compounds the dehydration and
  costs the sleep that jet lag needs. Not a moral point, just a poor trade.
- **Don't fast the flight to "reset".** Losing 1,500 kcal and 60 g of protein outweighs any
  circadian benefit in a lifting block.

### Jet lag and meal timing

Feeding time is a genuine circadian input, not just a consequence of one — it entrains
peripheral clocks in liver, gut and muscle even while the central clock is still on the old
timezone. Light and meal timing are the two levers with practical strength.

- **Switch meals to destination time on the first full day**, appetite or not. Breakfast at
  destination-morning is the strongest single anchor.
- **Get outdoor light in the destination morning.** Bigger effect than anything on the plate.
- **Eastward is harder than westward** — advancing the clock is worse than delaying it.
- Caffeine on destination-morning helps; after destination-2pm it delays adaptation.
- Appetite lags the clock by a couple of days. Under-eating for 48 h on arrival is normal and
  not worth fighting — hold protein and let calories catch up.

Training-side travel rules live in the `strength-conditioning-coach` skill, in its
`references/conditioning-and-recovery.md` §9.

## 3. Eating out and social events

No moralising here, and none in the reply. A restaurant meal, a takeaway, a wedding, a week in
Spain — these are what the training is for, not obstacles to it. The job is arithmetic and
ordering heuristics, delivered without commentary.

### Ordering by cuisine

| Cuisine | Order | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| **Italian** | Grilled meat or fish secondi + potatoes; simple tomato or ragù pasta | Cream sauces, garlic bread. A carbonara is ~1,200 kcal and 30 g protein |
| **Indian** | Tandoori or tikka chicken/paneer + plain rice + dal | Korma, masala, passanda — cream and ghee. Naan ~350 kcal each |
| **Thai / Chinese** | Steamed or stir-fried protein + steamed rice; beef and broccoli; larb | Deep-fried anything, sweet-and-sour, coconut curries, crispy duck |
| **Japanese** | Sashimi + rice, chicken or salmon teriyaki, edamame, gyudon | Tempura, katsu. Sushi is mostly rice — 8 pieces is ~20 g protein |
| **Mexican** | Burrito bowl or fajitas — grilled meat, rice, beans, salsa | Cheese, sour cream and chips are where the extra 800 kcal hides |
| **Pub** | Steak, gammon, grilled chicken, a burger. Chips are fine | Pies, batter, anything "loaded" |
| **Steakhouse** | Easiest venue here. 250-300 g sirloin + potatoes + veg = 60 g protein | Béarnaise and peppercorn, ~200 kcal a ladle |
| **Fast food** | Double burger no mayo, grilled chicken sandwich, chicken shop half-bird and rice | Milkshakes and large fries: 600-800 kcal, ~8 g protein |

Universal: order the largest lean protein on the menu, sauces on the side, a carb side rather
than a second fat side.

### Bank protein earlier in the day

The most effective technique for a known evening event. Nothing is restricted — the protein is
just moved to where it's controllable. 80 kg athlete, 160 g target, dinner out at 8pm:

```
08:00  300 g 0% Greek yoghurt + 60 g granola + 30 g whey       600 kcal ·  60 P
13:00  200 g chicken + 100 g dry rice + veg                     780 kcal ·  55 P
17:00  200 g cottage cheese                                     200 kcal ·  25 P
                                                    banked:   1,580 kcal · 140 P
20:00  Dinner out — whatever it is
```

By 5pm the day holds 140 g of protein with ~1,900 kcal of headroom. Dinner can be anything and
the day still lands. Far more reliable than ordering carefully at 8pm on an empty stomach.

### Multi-day events

Here's the maths. One estimated meal inside a well-logged week is noise — 30% wrong on a
1,200 kcal meal is 360 kcal, about 51 kcal/day across the week, inside the error bars on the
TDEE estimate itself. Seven of them is a trend: the same error becomes ~2,500 kcal a week,
roughly 0.3 kg of fat, and it repeats.

| Event | Approach |
|---|---|
| **One dinner** | Log an estimate, adjust nothing else. Genuinely ignore it |
| **A weekend** | Hold protein via breakfast and one banked feed; estimate the rest. Expect a 1-2 kg Monday jump from sodium, alcohol and carbs (§9) |
| **A week's holiday** | Drop the +300 surplus to maintenance, log estimates daily, keep one anchor — a high-protein breakfast every morning |
| **Two weeks or more** | As above, and expect the rate-of-gain read to be uninterpretable for the fortnight either side. Resume the trend line on return |

**The compensation trap:** skipping meals the day before or after an event is the one thing that
reliably makes it worse. It arrives hungry, and it disrupts two days of protein instead of none.

### Logging an unknown restaurant meal

Estimate high on fat. Restaurants cook in butter and oil in quantities no domestic kitchen uses,
and measured energy in restaurant meals runs well above stated values. So: estimate protein from
the menu portion (a chicken breast is ~180 g; a stated steak weight minus ~20% for trim and
cooking loss), estimate carbs from what's visible, then **add 20-30 g of fat** for the oil,
butter and dressing that isn't — about 200-270 kcal, and usually about right. Round up, not
down. An overestimate self-corrects through the guardrail in a fortnight; a systematic
underestimate looks like a broken TDEE model. `log_meal` with a plain description ("ribeye,
chips, peppercorn sauce, two pints") handles this well enough; a photo handles it better.

## 4. Alcohol

7 kcal/g, between carbohydrate and fat. On a +300 kcal surplus it isn't a catastrophe — it's a
line item that gets counted like any other. The reasons to *place* it carefully are
physiological, and worth stating accurately rather than dramatically.

**It isn't stored as fat efficiently, but that's not the reassurance it sounds like.** Ethanol
can't be stored, so the liver prioritises clearing it at a roughly fixed 7-10 g/hour regardless
of dose or activity. While that happens, fat oxidation is suppressed and the fat and carbs eaten
alongside get stored rather than burned. Net effect on energy balance: much the same as if the
alcohol calories had been fat.

**It blunts MPS at meaningful doses.** Parr et al. (2014) gave trained men ~1.5 g/kg after
concurrent training — about 120 g of ethanol, roughly 15 UK units at 80 kg. Myofibrillar MPS
fell ~37% with carbohydrate and ~24% with 25 g of protein co-ingested, for roughly 24 h. So
protein alongside reduces the effect without eliminating it. Two caveats worth keeping: that was
a heavy dose, and there was no low-dose arm. Don't extrapolate 37% to a pint.

**The sleep effect is the bigger practical problem.** Alcohol shortens sleep onset and raises
slow-wave sleep early, then suppresses REM and fragments the second half of the night (Ebrahim
et al. 2013) — at moderate doses, not just heavy ones. Sleep is the largest recovery lever
available, and a degraded night costs more in session quality and next-day appetite (§7, §8)
than the calories or the MPS effect do.

| Drink | kcal | Lower-calorie swap |
|---|---|---|
| Pint of lager (4.5%) | 210 | 330 ml bottle, 140 |
| Pint of stout (4.2%) | 190 | Already low for a pint |
| Pint of cider (4.5%) | 240 | Dry over sweet, ~200 |
| Craft IPA, 330 ml (6%) | 220 | Session IPA at 4%, ~140 |
| Alcohol-free beer, 330 ml | 60 | The best swap on this table |
| Wine, 175 ml (13%) | 150 | 125 ml pours, 105 |
| Wine, 250 ml (13%) | 215 | — |
| Bottle of wine (750 ml, 13%) | 640 | — |
| Prosecco / champagne, 125 ml | 90 | Lowest-calorie wine option |
| Single spirit + slimline mixer | 60 | Already the cheapest drink available |
| Gin & tonic (regular tonic) | 125 | Slimline tonic, ~60 |
| Espresso martini | 250 | — |
| Margarita / piña colada | 300-450 | Spirit and soda, ~60 |
| 4 pints over an evening | 800 | Alternate with soda water — halves it without halving the evening |

**Practical guidance.** Count it — untracked alcohol is the commonest reason a +300 surplus
behaves like +800. Keep it off the night before a heavy squat or deadlift session, and from
January off the night before a long run, where the sleep and hydration costs land directly on
the session. Eat 30-40 g of protein with or shortly after drinking. Alternate with water. Don't
compensate by skipping meals — skipping dinner to "make room" is the most reliable way to turn
four pints into a bad night and a worse next day. And a heavy night makes the next day a rest
day, not a write-off: shift the session rather than grinding it.

## 5. Illness

Three different illnesses, three different answers. The mistake is treating them the same.

### Fever and systemic illness

**Energy needs rise, they don't fall.** The long-standing clinical figure is a **10-13% increase
in resting metabolic rate per °C above normal** — 38.5 °C is ~15-20% above baseline, around
+300 kcal/day for this athlete before any activity. That's the opposite of what most people
assume, and it's why a week of flu can cost real lean mass rather than just training days.

- **Protein stays as high as appetite allows.** Systemic illness is catabolic — cytokine-driven
  proteolysis pulls amino acids out of muscle. If only one target gets hit, hit this one.
- **Don't chase the surplus.** Aim at maintenance and treat anything above it as a bonus.
- **Add 500-1,000 ml fluid** above the ~3 L baseline; fever raises sweat and respiratory losses.
- **Carbs over fat** — easier to digest, and the fever is drawing down glycogen.

A head cold without fever is barely a nutrition problem: normal targets, more fluid, appetite
down slightly for 2-3 days.

### GI illness — vomiting or diarrhoea

This one has a sequence, and skipping ahead in it causes the relapse.

| Phase | Timing | What |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Fluids and electrolytes** | 0-12 h | Small frequent sips. ORS, or 1 L water + 6 tsp sugar + ½ tsp salt. Rehydration is far more complete when the fluid contains sodium — plain water alone under-restores |
| **2. Simple carbs, low fibre, low fat** | 12-24 h | White rice, white toast, plain pasta, bananas, crackers, jelly. Fat and fibre both slow gastric emptying and provoke an irritable gut |
| **3. Add protein** | 24-48 h | Chicken breast, white fish, eggs, whey in water. Start at ~20 g a feed, not 40 |
| **4. Normal** | 48-72 h | Fat back first, then fibre, then dairy last |

Dairy is often temporarily poorly tolerated after gastroenteritis — transient lactase reduction
is common and resolves in a week or two. Don't diagnose an intolerance from it.

### When nothing appeals

| Food | kcal | P | C | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 g whey in 300 ml water | 120 | 25 | 2 | 2 |
| 250 g 0% Greek yoghurt + 20 g honey | 210 | 25 | 25 | 1 |
| 500 ml chocolate milk | 320 | 17 | 45 | 8 |
| Chicken noodle soup + 100 g shredded chicken | 350 | 35 | 25 | 10 |
| 250 g white rice + 100 g plain chicken | 400 | 35 | 55 | 4 |
| Instant oat sachet + 20 g whey in water | 300 | 25 | 38 | 5 |
| Smoothie: banana, 250 ml milk, 30 g whey | 400 | 35 | 45 | 8 |
| Tinned peaches + 200 g cottage cheese | 300 | 26 | 35 | 4 |
| 2 scrambled eggs + white toast | 320 | 18 | 25 | 16 |
| Electrolyte drink, 500 ml | 120 | 0 | 30 | 0 |

Cold and bland beats hot and seasoned when nausea is the problem; liquids beat solids. A whey
shake and a bowl of white rice is a perfectly good illness day.

### Don't panic-adjust the plan

**Weight loss during illness is fluid and gut contents, not muscle.** Three days of GI illness
routinely shows 2-3 kg, and most of it returns within 48 h of eating and drinking normally.
Suspend the rate-of-gain read for the illness week and the week after; don't change the calorie
target on an illness weigh-in; and don't try to catch up with three 4,500 kcal days afterwards.
Return to the normal target and let the trend line resume.

For restarting training, nutrition follows the `strength-conditioning-coach` skill's
**return-to-training ladder** (its `references/conditioning-and-recovery.md` §8) — four sessions
at ~50/70/85/100% of prescribed volume, each gated on symptoms not returning. Carbs scale with the rung; protein
stays at full target throughout.

## 6. Appetite crashed

Can't hit the calorie target. Classic timing: **weeks 3-4 of a mesocycle**, where accumulated
volume and fatigue peak, and **hot weather**, where thermoregulation suppresses appetite
directly. Also common for a fortnight after any increase in the surplus, while the gut adapts.

The primary lever is **calorie density** — kcal per unit of stomach volume. Everything else is
secondary.

| Food | kcal/100 g | P | C | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Olive oil | 884 | 0 | 0 | 100 |
| Mixed nuts | 620 | 20 | 12 | 55 |
| Peanut / almond butter | 600 | 25 | 20 | 50 |
| Dark chocolate (70%) | 600 | 8 | 45 | 42 |
| Trail mix | 480 | 12 | 45 | 28 |
| Granola | 450 | 10 | 65 | 16 |
| Flapjack | 450 | 5 | 60 | 20 |
| Cheddar | 400 | 25 | 0 | 33 |
| Oats (dry) | 380 | 13 | 60 | 7 |
| White rice (dry) | 360 | 7 | 79 | 1 |
| Honey / maple syrup | 300 | 0 | 78 | 0 |
| Dried dates / raisins | 290 | 2 | 70 | 0 |
| Bagel | 270 | 11 | 53 | 2 |
| Avocado | 160 | 2 | 9 | 15 |
| Whole milk (per 100 ml) | 64 | 3.4 | 4.7 | 3.6 |

**Fat is the cheapest calorie lever there is.** A tablespoon of olive oil over food already
cooked is 120 kcal with no added volume and no extra chewing; four of those across the day is
480 kcal nobody notices. Same logic for 30 g of nut butter in porridge (180 kcal), and for
switching every low-fat product back to the full-fat version.

**Liquid calories** bypass most of the satiety response solid food produces — a problem in §7
and exactly the point here.

| Shake | kcal | P | C | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 g oats, 400 ml whole milk, 40 g whey, 40 g peanut butter, banana | 1,140 | 71 | 114 | 44 |
| 300 ml whole milk, 40 g whey, 30 g almond butter, 40 g maltodextrin | 690 | 50 | 57 | 29 |
| 60 g oats, 250 ml whole milk, 30 g whey, 20 g honey | 560 | 42 | 67 | 16 |
| Dairy-free: 400 ml soya milk, 40 g pea isolate, 60 g oats, 30 g peanut butter | 740 | 60 | 56 | 29 |

The 690 kcal one is deliberately low-volume — about 400 ml in the glass. That's the one for
when even a full shaker feels like too much.

**The other levers:** drop fibre to ~25 g for a week or two (white rice over brown, peeled
potatoes, juice over whole fruit) — temporary, not a new default; cut the low-calorie volume,
because a 300 g salad and a litre of diet soft drink occupy space that has to be paid for later;
move water between meals rather than immediately before them; six feeds of 550 kcal instead of
four of 850, with the 0.4 g/kg per-feed protein logic intact; and **eat the protein first** when
a meal is a struggle — protein is the one macro that can't be recovered later with a spoonful of
oil. Warm, salty, savoury usually goes down better than sweet, and in a heatwave cold beats hot
— a rice salad works where a stew doesn't.

**When it means something else.** A persistently absent appetite for more than two weeks is a
signal, not a nuisance.

| Signal | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Appetite down + performance down + poor sleep + resting HR up | Overreaching | Deload now. Tell `strength-conditioning-coach` |
| Appetite down + low-grade fatigue + sore throat | Illness incubating | Treat as §5. Take the day |
| Appetite down on training days only | Session too close to meals | Move meals, not calories |
| Appetite fine at maintenance, absent at target | The surplus is genuinely too large | Cut to +150-200 and recheck the rate of gain in 3 weeks. A surplus that can't be eaten isn't a surplus |
| Appetite down in hot weather only | Thermoregulation | Cold, liquid, salty. It resolves with the weather |

## 7. Appetite runaway

Eating past the target consistently. The levers invert almost exactly.

**Protein first.** It's the most satiating macro per calorie by a wide margin — raising protein
to ~30% of energy cut spontaneous intake substantially in controlled feeding (Weigle et al.
2005). At 160 g on 3,500 kcal the athlete is at ~18%, so front-loading it (50-60 g at breakfast)
does more than the daily total does. **Fibre and food volume second** — 35-40 g of fibre and
physically large, low-density meals. Boiled potatoes topped the classic satiety-index work per
calorie, with whole foods generally above processed ones (Holt et al. 1995).

| Food | kcal | P | C | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 300 g boiled potatoes | 240 | 6 | 54 | 0 |
| 250 g 0% Greek yoghurt | 145 | 25 | 10 | 0 |
| 200 g white fish | 210 | 46 | 0 | 2 |
| 200 g chicken breast | 330 | 62 | 0 | 8 |
| 200 g cottage cheese | 195 | 24 | 8 | 5 |
| 3 boiled eggs | 235 | 19 | 1 | 17 |
| 250 g porridge (water) + 30 g whey | 340 | 33 | 45 | 6 |
| 400 g broth-based vegetable soup | 160 | 8 | 22 | 4 |
| 250 g cooked lentils | 290 | 22 | 45 | 2 |
| 50 g air-popped popcorn | 190 | 6 | 38 | 2 |
| 2 apples | 190 | 1 | 50 | 0 |
| 300 g mixed veg or a large salad | 90 | 5 | 15 | 1 |

**Liquid calories are the enemy here.** The same low satiety per calorie that makes the §6 shake
useful makes it the first thing to remove when the problem is overshooting (DiMeglio & Mattes
2000). Juice, smoothies, sugary coffees and mass-gainer shakes register barely at all. Eat the
protein, don't drink it.

**Hunger, habit, or something else.** Genuine hunger builds gradually, is felt physically, and
is satisfied by any food. Habit and stress eating arrive suddenly, attach to a specific food,
and are tied to a context — 9pm on the sofa, the walk past the kitchen, the third hour of a bad
meeting. Two diagnostics that work: *would an apple and 200 g of Greek yoghurt do?* (if no, it
isn't hunger), and *what happened in the 20 minutes before this started?* The fixes for habit
eating are environmental, not nutritional — change the context, move the trigger food out of the
house, put a protein-forward option where the habit reaches. No willpower framing, no moral one.

**Sleep debt.** Restricting healthy young men to 4 h in bed for two nights lowered leptin,
raised ghrelin and increased hunger and appetite, with the largest increases for calorie-dense
carbohydrate foods (Spiegel et al. 2004). This is hormonal, not a character defect, and it's the
commonest single explanation for a week of overshooting every day. Ask about sleep before
adjusting anything on the plate — fixing the sleep fixes the appetite, while fighting appetite
with willpower on 5 h a night fixes nothing. See §8.

**When runaway appetite is the correct signal.** Sometimes appetite isn't the problem, the
target is. Recalculate rather than resist when:

- **Training load has genuinely increased** — more sets, more sessions, a jump in step count,
  or (from January) running back in the week. The TDEE model is now wrong and the body is
  correcting it. Check `get_block_status()` and ask `strength-conditioning-coach` what changed.
- **Weight is flat or falling despite hitting the target.** The clearest possible evidence the
  target is too low. Verify a week of honest logging, then +300 kcal per `lean-bulk-protocol.md`
  §4.
- **A deload just ended** — appetite lags training volume by about a week in both directions.
- **Body composition is fine.** Weight up 0.4%/week, body fat flat, loads progressing — that's
  the plan working. Don't fix it.
- **NEAT has fallen** (a desk-heavy fortnight, an injury, bad weather). Here the target is too
  *high* and the appetite is unrelated — which is why diagnosis comes before adjustment.

## 8. Hangovers, bad sleep, high-stress weeks

The unifying principle: **in a bad week the goal is maintenance and an unbroken log, not
progress.** No surplus chasing, no PB attempts, no elaborate plan. A block is 22 weeks long; one
bad week costs almost nothing unless it takes the structure down with it.

### The hangover sequence

Ethanol clears at 7-10 g/hour and nothing accelerates it. What can be improved is everything
around it — dehydration, electrolyte loss, low blood glucose, disrupted sleep.

1. **Fluids and electrolytes first.** 500-750 ml with sodium: ORS, a sports drink, or salted
   soup. Alcohol suppresses vasopressin, so the deficit is real, and sodium is what makes the
   fluid stay in. Plain water alone works less well.
2. **Carbs next.** Toast, oats, juice, honey. Gluconeogenesis is suppressed while ethanol is
   being cleared, and a decent share of "hangover" is that.
3. **Protein once food is tolerated.** 30-40 g — eggs, yoghurt, a shake. The day's most useful
   macro given §4.
4. **Eat normally the rest of the day.** Skipping meals to offset last night is two disrupted
   days for the price of one. Caffeine is fine, but it isn't fluid replacement.

**"Sweating it out" is a bad idea.** Ethanol elimination is hepatic and effectively fixed;
exercise doesn't speed it up. What a hard session on a hangover does deliver is a larger fluid
deficit, elevated heart rate, degraded coordination, and a poor-quality session that still costs
recovery. If anything happens, make it a walk, easy cycling, or the aerobic maintenance session
that was already scheduled.

### Bad sleep

Short or broken sleep raises appetite (§7), erodes adherence to anything requiring decisions,
and degrades session quality. Nutritionally:

- **Front-load protein** — 50-60 g at breakfast. The single most effective intervention on a
  low-sleep day: it blunts the appetite curve before it starts and banks the macro most likely
  to be missed.
- **Don't fight appetite with willpower alone.** Restructure — high-satiety options where the
  reaching happens (§7), liquid calories out, volume added to meals. Expect to be over target,
  and accept it. One day over is noise.
- **Cap caffeine early** — nothing after ~2pm, roughly 8-10 h before bed. Its half-life is ~5 h.
  The temptation is a fourth coffee at 4pm, which buys a second bad night, which is how one bad
  night becomes a bad week.
- **Don't use alcohol to get to sleep** (§4). Carbs at the evening meal are mildly helpful for
  sleep onset and cost nothing on a bulk.

### High-stress weeks

Deadline weeks, family crises, a house move. The failure mode isn't a bad meal — it's the whole
structure collapsing because it needed more attention than the week had spare. **Simplify to
three fixed anchor meals and drop every optimisation.**

```
07:30  Same breakfast, every day    ~600 kcal · 55 P   (yoghurt + granola + whey, 3 min)
13:00  Same lunch, every day        ~780 kcal · 55 P   (prepped chicken + rice, reheated)
19:30  Whatever dinner happens      estimate and log; close any gap with a shake
```

Drop meal timing, carb cycling and the pre/post-session refinements — they're worth single-digit
percentages and cost attention this week doesn't have. Protect protein and sleep; those two
carry most of the outcome. Keep training at reduced volume (that's `strength-conditioning-coach`
territory) with carbs at the rest-day number on light days. And **keep logging even when the log
is ugly** — an honest week of 2,800 kcal with three estimated dinners is far more useful than a
blank one, because it's the only thing that makes the weight trend interpretable afterwards.
Expect a flat or slightly-down week. That's the correct outcome for a stress week, not a
failure, and not a reason to touch the surplus.

## 9. Weight-trend anomalies

Daily bodyweight moves 1-2 kg on things unrelated to fat or muscle. Almost every "I've gained
1.5 kg overnight" and "I'm losing weight on a surplus" message is on this table. Diagnose before
adjusting anything.

| Observation | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **+1-2 kg overnight** | Sodium, a large carb load, or both. Each gram of stored glycogen binds ~3 g water (Olsson & Saltin 1970) — a 400 g carb refeed can add 1.5 kg of water alone | Nothing. Resolves in 24-72 h. Do not adjust calories |
| **+1-1.5 kg in the first 1-2 weeks of a surplus** | Glycogen, its bound water, and more food in transit. Not tissue | Expected. `lean-bulk-protocol.md` §4 rule 5 — don't cut the surplus over it |
| **+~1 kg in the first 2-4 weeks of creatine** | Intracellular water; creatine is osmotically active | Note the start date, discount ~1 kg, read the slope from week 4 |
| **+0.5-1 kg after a large or high-fibre meal the night before** | Gut contents. A 1 kg meal weighs 1 kg | Re-weigh in 48 h |
| **Weight up, no bowel movement for 2-3 days** | Constipation from travel, low fibre, disrupted routine — genuinely 0.5-1.5 kg | Fluid, fibre, movement. Not a calorie adjustment |
| **Weight down 1-2 kg, dark urine, after a flight or hot week** | Dehydration reading as loss | Rehydrate and re-weigh. The commonest cause of a confusing trend |
| **+1-1.5 kg after an unusually hard or novel session** | DOMS and the inflammatory response — local fluid retention and muscle swelling | Resolves in 3-5 days. Common in a mesocycle's first week |
| **Weight up the morning after drinking** | Sodium from the food alongside, plus rebound retention after alcohol's diuretic phase | 48-72 h. Not fat — 4 pints is ~800 kcal, ~0.1 kg at most |
| **Weight up after a restaurant or takeaway meal** | Sodium-driven water retention; restaurant meals routinely carry 3-5 g of sodium | 24-48 h |
| **Weight up pre-menstrually (where relevant)** | Luteal-phase fluid retention, commonly 0.5-2 kg | Compare like phase with like phase across cycles, not week with week |
| **Flat during illness, then a 2 kg drop** | Fluid and gut contents (§5) | Suspend the trend read for that week and the next |
| **Flat 3+ weeks, loads progressing** | Possible recomp — lean mass up, fat down | Check body-fat % and loads before touching calories. May be a good outcome |
| **Flat 3+ weeks, loads stalling** | Genuine under-fuelling, or under-reported intake | Verify a week of complete logging, then +300 kcal. Tell `strength-conditioning-coach` not to add volume yet |

**The rule that ties them together.** Any one of these moves the scale 1-2 kg, and the target
rate of gain at 80 kg is 200-400 g/week. So a single water-weight event can mask — or
fabricate — **three to five weeks** of genuine trend. That is precisely why the guardrail in
`lean-bulk-protocol.md` §4 uses a **3-4 week rolling average** and never a single weigh-in, and
why an adjustment gets 2-3 weeks before it's judged.

Hygiene that removes most of the noise for free: same time of day, first thing, post-bathroom,
pre-food, minimal clothing; daily weigh-ins read only as a weekly average, or weekly weigh-ins
if daily numbers cause anxiety — same conclusion, slower. `get_body_metrics({weeks: 4})` returns
the trend; **use the trend line, not the endpoints**, because picking the two ends of a 4-week
window is exactly how a water-weight day becomes a calorie adjustment. When a number doesn't
fit, ask three things before doing anything: what did you eat last night, how much did you
drink, and how did you sleep.

## 10. Evidence notes

- **Parr et al. (2014)**, *PLoS ONE* — ~1.5 g/kg alcohol after concurrent training suppressed
  myofibrillar protein synthesis ~37% with carbohydrate and ~24% with 25 g protein co-ingested.
- **Ebrahim et al. (2013)**, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* — alcohol shortens sleep onset and increases
  slow-wave sleep early, then suppresses REM and fragments the second half of the night.
- **Spiegel et al. (2004)**, *Ann Intern Med* — two nights of 4 h in bed lowered leptin, raised
  ghrelin, and increased hunger and appetite, especially for calorie-dense carbohydrate foods.
- **Gorissen et al. (2018)**, *Amino Acids* — amino acid composition of commercial plant protein
  isolates; leucine and EAA fractions generally below animal sources.
- **van Vliet, Burd & van Loon (2015)**, *J Nutr* — muscle anabolic response to plant- vs
  animal-based protein; digestibility and amino acid profile as the mechanisms.
- **Burke et al. (2003)**, *Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab* — vegetarians had lower baseline muscle
  creatine and larger increases with supplementation than omnivores.
- **Hurrell & Egli (2010)**, *Am J Clin Nutr* — non-haem iron bioavailability; ascorbic acid
  enhances absorption, tea and coffee polyphenols inhibit it.
- **Bath et al. (2017)**, *Br J Nutr* — iodine in UK milk-alternative drinks is a small fraction
  of that in cow's milk.
- **Weigle et al. (2005)**, *Am J Clin Nutr* — raising protein from 15% to 30% of energy at ad
  libitum intake reduced spontaneous energy intake and bodyweight.
- **Holt et al. (1995)**, *Eur J Clin Nutr* — satiety index of common foods; boiled potatoes
  highest per calorie, whole foods generally above processed.
- **DiMeglio & Mattes (2000)**, *Int J Obes* — liquid carbohydrate produced weaker dietary
  compensation than the same energy eaten as solid food.
- **Olsson & Saltin (1970)**, *Acta Physiol Scand* — total body water tracks muscle glycogen;
  the basis of the ~3 g water per gram of glycogen figure.
