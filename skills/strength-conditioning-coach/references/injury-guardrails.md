# Injury Guardrails

These are hard constraints on exercise selection and progression. They override requests for
more volume, more range, or heavier loading. When a guardrail blocks something the athlete
wants, say so plainly and give the substitution — don't quietly drop the exercise.

Active flags for this athlete: `highHamstring`, `knee`, `tightHips`, `ankleMobility`.

---

## 1. Proximal ("high") hamstring strain / tendinopathy risk

### The conflict, stated openly

Goal 3 is to grow hamstrings. The most effective hamstring hypertrophy stimulus is loading in a
lengthened position — deficit RDLs, stiff-leg deadlifts, seated leg curls. That is precisely the
position that provokes a proximal hamstring tendon: compression of the tendon against the
ischial tuberosity under load at deep hip flexion.

The resolution is **sequencing, not avoidance**. Hamstrings still get built — later in the block,
at range, once load tolerance has been established at mid-range. Early hamstring volume is
deliberately held at 10-14 sets/week rather than the 16-20 a priority muscle would otherwise get.
That volume is redirected to glutes, which is priority 1 of the posterior chain anyway.

### The progression model

Standard proximal hamstring tendinopathy rehab progresses through four stages (Goom, Malliaras
& Reiman 2016). Map every prescription onto a stage:

**Stage 1 — Isometrics, hip near neutral.**
- Long-lever bridge hold, single-leg bridge hold, isometric hamstring push into a bench.
- 5 × 30-45s, daily or every other day. Isometrics are analgesic for tendon pain and build
  tolerance without tendon compression.
- Exit criteria: pain-free holds at bodyweight, no next-day soreness.

**Stage 2 — Mid-range concentric/eccentric, hip extended.**
- **Lying** leg curl, cable pull-through, 45° back extension to horizontal only, hip thrust,
  glute bridge, mid-range RDL from a standing (not deficit) position with a shortened ROM.
- Load progresses here. Range does not.
- Exit criteria: 3-4 weeks of stable load progression, working pain ≤3/10, nothing lingering.

**Stage 3 — Progressive range under load.**
- Add ~2-3 cm of ROM to hinge patterns per week, as tolerated. Full-ROM RDL, single-leg RDL,
  Nordic eccentrics with generous assistance.
- Seated leg curl may enter here — cautiously, partial range first.
- Exit criteria: full-range hinge at working loads, symptom-free for 2 weeks.

**Stage 4 — Energy storage / lengthened loading.**
- Deficit RDL, good morning, stiff-leg deadlift, full-range seated leg curl, and later
  running-specific higher-speed work.
- This is late block, and it is the bridge to January's return to running.

### The seated vs. lying leg curl distinction

This one matters and gets missed constantly:

- **Seated leg curl** — hip is flexed ~90°. The proximal hamstring origin is already lengthened
  before the set starts, and the tendon is compressed against the ischial tuberosity while
  under load. **Not early.** Stage 3 at the earliest.
- **Lying (prone) leg curl** — hip is extended. The proximal attachment stays short; the work
  happens at the knee. **This is the safe early choice** and it is the default hamstring
  movement for the first half of the block.

Seated leg curl is the better hypertrophy stimulus in a healthy athlete (greater growth in the
lengthened position). That's exactly why it's dangerous here, and exactly why it's the reward
for reaching stage 3 rather than a starting point.

### Load before range

Non-negotiable rule: **progress load before range.** Add range only as tolerated, and only after
load progression has been stable for at least 2-3 weeks with no symptom carry-over. Never add
load and range in the same week.

If range increases and symptoms appear, roll back to the last symptom-free range and hold there
for two weeks before trying again — don't drop the load as well, or the tendon detrains.

### Pain-monitoring rule

The rule that governs every hamstring decision:

- **≤3/10 during the working set, settled by the next morning → acceptable.** Continue as
  programmed.
- **>3/10 during the set, OR any pain that lingers into the next day, OR morning stiffness at
  the ischial tuberosity → regress.** Drop load 10-15% or reduce range to the last comfortable
  position. Re-test in one session.
- **Pain on sitting, or a sharp deep-buttock pain radiating down the back of the thigh →
  stop loading the hinge entirely** and revert to stage 1 isometrics. This is the classic
  proximal tendinopathy presentation.

Ask about pain explicitly when writing sessions. Don't wait to be told.

### Movement selection rules

- **Hip thrusts and glute bridges are the early glute driver.** They produce very high glute
  activation and grow glutes hard while the hip stays near neutral at the point of peak tension
  — minimal proximal hamstring lengthening. This is why the split leads with hip thrust twice a
  week.
- **Favour single-leg and staggered variants at tolerable range** over heavy bilateral stretched
  loading in the early weeks. B-stance RDL, single-leg hip thrust, staggered-stance pull-through:
  lower absolute load on the tendon, similar stimulus per limb, and they double as left/right
  correctives.
- 45° back extensions are fine **to horizontal only** early. Going below horizontal with a
  rounded start puts the proximal tendon into stretch under load.

### Stage gates, made explicit

Stage advancement is a decision, not a calendar event. Each gate has to be met on all counts, and
the athlete has to have been *tested* against it rather than assumed to pass. When asked "can I
move to stage 3", check these and answer with the specific unmet criterion if the answer is no.

| Gate | Criteria (all must hold) |
|---|---|
| **1 → 2** | Pain-free long-lever bridge holds at bodyweight, 5 × 30s. No next-morning soreness at the ischial tuberosity. No pain on prolonged sitting |
| **2 → 3** | 3-4 consecutive weeks of load progression at capped range. Working pain never above 3/10 and always settled by the next morning. No pain on sitting. Lying leg curl progressing normally |
| **3 → 4** | Full-range hinge at working loads, symptom-free for 2 consecutive weeks. Single-leg RDL at bodyweight-plus with no end-range pain. Seated leg curl tolerated at partial range |
| **4 → return to running** | See below — this gate is shared with `endurance-running-coach` |

### Return-to-running criteria (shared gate)

The block's whole purpose, tissue-wise, is to arrive at January with a proximal hamstring that
tolerates lengthened loading at speed. Running — particularly faster running and downhills —
loads the proximal tendon in exactly the position the block has been protecting, so the return
is the single highest-risk moment in the whole plan.

This skill **owns and reports** the loading criteria. `endurance-running-coach` **owns the
return-to-run decision and its progression.** Neither should make the other's call.

Report these when asked whether the hamstring is ready:

1. **Stage 4 loading tolerated.** Deficit RDL, good morning, or full-range seated leg curl at
   working loads, symptom-free for 2+ weeks.
2. **Symmetry.** Single-leg bridge or leg-curl strength within ~10% left-to-right. A limb deficit
   at the point of return is a well-established reinjury risk factor.
3. **No pain on prolonged sitting**, and no morning stiffness at the ischial tuberosity.
4. **Eccentric capacity.** Nordic curls or slow single-leg RDL eccentrics tolerated at full range
   without provocation.
5. **Nothing lingering.** Four consecutive weeks with no working pain above 2/10 in any hinge or
   curl pattern.

If any of the five is unmet, say which one and what would close it. Do not soften the report
because the calendar says January.

### Substitution table

| Wanted | Early-block substitute | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Seated leg curl | Lying leg curl | Hip extended, no proximal compression |
| Deficit RDL | Standing RDL, shortened ROM | Removes the lengthened end-range |
| Stiff-leg deadlift | Trap-bar RDL, mid-range | Less hip flexion at the bottom |
| Good morning | Cable pull-through | Same hinge, load vector doesn't stretch under tension |
| Nordic curl | Lying leg curl eccentric (3-4s) | Eccentric stimulus without max lengthened load |
| Conventional deadlift | Rack pull from knee, or hip thrust | Reduces start-position hip flexion |
| Below-horizontal back extension | Back extension to horizontal | Caps the range |
| Heavy bilateral RDL | B-stance RDL | Lower absolute tendon load per side |

## 2. Knee issues

### Principles

- **Manage deep-knee-flexion volume**, don't eliminate knee flexion. Tendons need load; what
  they don't tolerate well is high volume at deep flexion under load.
- **Grow quads through leg press and hack squat in a pain-free ROM** rather than forcing squat
  depth. Quads don't care whether the ROM came from a barbell.
- **Tempo and tendon-friendly progressions:** slow eccentrics (3-4s), isometric holds, and
  tolerable-ROM work. Isometric quad loading (Spanish squat, wall sit, leg-extension holds) is
  analgesic for patellar tendon pain and is the first thing to reach for on a flare.

### Tools

| Tool | Use |
|---|---|
| Spanish squat | Band behind knees, upright torso. Isometric or slow reps. Knee-friendly quad loading |
| Leg extension, partial ROM | Start from the range that's pain-free (often mid-to-top), expand over weeks |
| Heel-elevated squat | Shifts demand, reduces required ankle DF, keeps torso upright |
| Leg press, pain-free depth | Highest-tolerance quad builder. Set the stop where it's comfortable |
| Hack squat, depth to tolerance | Supported, so depth can be limited without balance compromise |
| Slow eccentrics (3-4s) | Tendon adaptation without peak load |
| Step-downs, controlled | Eccentric knee control, low load |

### Rules

- Pain-free ROM is the working ROM. Expand it gradually over the block; don't chase depth as a
  goal in itself.
- Same pain rule as the hamstring: ≤3/10 during, settled next day, is acceptable.
- If knee pain flares, drop to isometrics (Spanish squat holds, leg extension isometrics) for a
  week rather than skipping lower-body work entirely. Detraining a painful tendon makes it worse.
- Avoid high-volume deep-flexion clusters — e.g. don't stack deep leg press, ATG split squats,
  and deep hack squats in one session.

## 3. Tight hips + limited ankle dorsiflexion

### Why it gets a dedicated block, not a warm-up

Ankle dorsiflexion restriction is the mechanical limiter on squat depth: without ~35-40° of DF,
the knee can't travel forward far enough for an upright deep squat, so the athlete compensates
with excessive forward lean, heel lift, or lumbar flexion. Tight hip flexors do the same thing
from above, limiting hip extension at the top of hip thrusts and stealing glute range.

Both directly limit goals 1-3. That's why mobility is goal 5 and not an afterthought.

### The mobility block (front-loaded, every session, 6-8 min)

**Ankle dorsiflexion**
- Knee-to-wall ankle rocks — 2 × 10/side. Also the progress test: measure toe-to-wall distance.
- Loaded dorsiflexion hold in a deep split — 2 × 30s/side, drive the knee over the toe.
- Weighted calf-raise loaded stretch at the bottom — 2 × 20s. Loaded end-range beats passive
  stretch for retained range.

**Hips**
- Half-kneeling hip flexor stretch with posterior pelvic tilt — 2 × 30s/side. Squeeze the
  glute of the trailing leg; without that it's just a lumbar extension stretch.
- 90-90 transitions — 8 slow reps. Internal + external rotation.
- Adductor rock-back — 2 × 30s/side.
- Deep-squat prep: goblet squat hold, heels elevated, 2 × 30s, prying at the bottom.

### Programming rules

- **Heel elevation is the default** on all squat patterns, not a remedial option. It removes
  the DF requirement so the quads get trained while the ankle is being worked on separately.
- **Depth to tolerance.** Never grind for depth the ankle can't give — that's how the lumbar
  spine ends up absorbing it.
- Load end-range where possible. Passive static stretching produces short-lived range; loaded
  end-range work (weighted DF holds, loaded calf stretch, deep goblet holds) retains it.
- Calf raise work through **full range with a pause at the bottom** doubles as calf hypertrophy
  (priority 3) and DF mobility. Two targets, one movement.

### Tracking adherence

- Ask after each session whether the mobility block was completed, and log it.
- Report weekly adherence. If it's under ~75%, cut the block to three drills — a shorter block
  that gets done beats a complete one that gets skipped.
- **Progress marker: knee-to-wall distance in cm, both sides, measured every 2 weeks.** Report
  the trend. That number predicts whether squat depth will improve; nothing else does.
- Hip extension check: Thomas test position every 4 weeks, or simply note whether full lockout
  at the top of the hip thrust is achievable without lumbar compensation.

## 4. Red flags — stop and refer

Programming stops and the athlete sees a physio if any of these appear:

- Sharp deep-buttock pain radiating down the posterior thigh, worse on sitting — proximal
  hamstring tendinopathy that has progressed beyond load management.
- Any sudden "pop" or acute onset during a hinge, with weakness or bruising.
- Knee locking, giving way, or true joint-line swelling.
- Numbness, tingling, or pins and needles in the leg — that's neural, not muscular, and load
  management won't fix it.
- Night pain, or pain unrelated to loading.
- Pain that doesn't settle after two consecutive deloaded sessions.

Say this plainly when it applies. Don't program through it.

## 5. Evidence base

- **Goom, Malliaras & Reiman (2016)**, *Proximal Hamstring Tendinopathy: Clinical Aspects of
  Assessment and Management*, JOSPT — the four-stage isometric → mid-range → range → energy-storage
  progression used above, and the tendon-compression-at-hip-flexion mechanism.
- **Cook & Purdam (2009)** — tendon continuum model; isometric loading for pain modulation.
- **Maeo et al. (2021)** — greater hamstring hypertrophy from seated (lengthened) vs lying leg
  curl in healthy lifters. This is why seated curl is the stage-3 reward, not the starting point.
- **Contreras et al.** — hip thrust EMG and hypertrophy work supporting the thrust as a
  high-glute-stimulus, low-hamstring-length movement.
- **Rio et al. (2015)** — isometric contractions reduce patellar tendon pain immediately and
  for ~45 min after, supporting Spanish squats and leg-extension isometrics on knee flare days.
- **Macrum et al. (2012)** — restricted ankle dorsiflexion alters squat mechanics (increased
  knee valgus, reduced quadriceps activation), supporting heel elevation as a default rather
  than a crutch.
